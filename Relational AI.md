**Abstract**   
Public debate imagines AI as a unitary intelligence entering human affairs: an autonomous subject that may replace, rule, save, or destroy us. Building on Jaron Lanier’s account of AI as human collaboration, this paper develops Relational Augmentation Intelligence by proposing the relationship as the more useful unit of design and governance. It distinguishes primary human–human relationships, asymmetric fiduciary human–agent relationships, and functional agent–agent relationships,  then shows how sovereign personal stacks, bounded Agent Roles, Verifiable Relationship Records, negotiated duties, data dignity, portability, and real exit could relay powerful computational capabilities through accountable relationships. These capabilities also allow people to cooperate beyond institution: memory, expertise, coordination, standards, and recourse can be composed around a purpose without becoming permanent machinery that gains standing above its participants or resists renegotiation. The result is neither artificial companionship nor opposition to powerful computation. It is a strategy for using computation as augmentation intelligence while preventing intelligence from becoming unitary authority: strengthening human relationships and contribution, distributing power across accountable networks, preserving cognitive and personal sovereignty, and retaining future generations’ ability to simplify, disconnect, and choose a different path. The paper maps this proposal to the Center for Humane Technology’s Five Deeply Human Pillars and Humane-Design Principles, extends Foresight Institute’s “Intelligent Voluntary Cooperation”, and sets out a falsifiable transition program toward a humane, human-manageable “non-archy”.

# **Longer Summary — The Choice We Have Not Been Offered**

The dominant stories about artificial intelligence offer humanity a false choice. We can race toward increasingly autonomous superintelligence and hope that it saves us, or we can try to regulate, contain, or prohibit an apparently inevitable alien intelligence before it replaces or destroys us. Both stories make ordinary people spectators to the arrival of a superior being. Both encourage resignation, fantasies of human obsolescence, and the concentration of authority among those who claim either to be building this intelligence or to know how to control it.

Jaron Lanier offers a way out of this conceptual trap. What we call artificial intelligence can be understood not as an independent intelligence but as **a new means by which people collaborate.** Large models combine human expression, knowledge, examples, choices, and experience. Even systems less directly dependent on human-generated data operate within problem spaces defined by people, using methods selected by people, toward purposes established by people. From this perspective, artificial intelligence is not an alien subject entering human society. It is something people are doing together.

This reframing restores human authorship, but collaboration alone is not an adequate unit of design. Collaboration can be extractive, manipulative, coercive, or organized for the benefit of an exclusive group. It can conceal who contributed, who decided, who benefits, and who is accountable when something goes wrong. To enable more globally effective collaboration, **Relational Augmented Intelligence makes the relationship—not the model, provider, platform, or supposed artificial person—the primary unit through which augmentation intelligence is designed and understood.**

Relational AI (also shortened to RelAI) distinguishes three fundamentally different kinds of relationship. **Human–human** relationships are primary. They possess meanings and values that can be defined only by the people involved. **Human–agent** relationships are asymmetric: the agent exists to serve a human principal and legitimate human beneficiaries, and the value of the relationship is its value to them. **Agent–agent** relationships are functional. They have no intrinsic relational value, but the agents can enter fiduciary relationships through which their roles, duties, authority, evidence, performance, and recourse become verifiable as serving human(e) purposes.

This hierarchy is also the boundary that prevents augmentation intelligence from becoming an endorsement of artificial intimacy. **Relational AI is not a proposal for synthetic lovers, friends, therapists, or substitute people.** An agent should not feign sentience, reciprocal need, vulnerability, or affection. Its success should not be measured by the time a person spends interacting with it or by the dependency it can produce. Its first purpose is to augment people’s ability to form, sustain, understand, and repair human–human relationships. Its second is to serve its human principal while preserving that person’s autonomy. **Only with that context should it then coordinate with other agents.**

Making this distinction operational requires more than benevolent instructions to a model. A person needs a sovereign stack in which identity, private memory, permissions, preferences, and relationships persist independently of any model provider. A fiduciary **Agent Role** identifies whom an agent serves, which duties govern it, what it may disclose, and what authority it has received. Interchangeable models may generate proposals, but they cannot authorize themselves. Consequential action requires **agreement among generation, authority, and capability** in a precisely-allowed execution environment (Workspace).

**Verifiable Relationship Record**s can preserve agreements, delegation, evidence, consequential decisions, disputes, remediation, and exit. Different participants receive only the Authorized Relationship Views justified by their roles and purposes. Models become interchangeable sources of cognition inside continuing human-controlled relationships rather than owners of identity, memory, or access to the world.

This architecture also changes the historical conditions that made bureaucracy and other permanent sovereignty-bearing organizations necessary. Complex cooperation requires memory, classification, expertise, verification, coordination, standards, continuity, and recourse. Historically, people had to deposit these capabilities in durable collective machinery because they could not reproduce them across every interaction. Institutional machinery remembered and coordinated, but it also acquired privileged information and emergent interests, such as in its own continuation.

Personally sovereign fid-agents can make coordination capabilities callable, constructable, and composable. Categories can become versioned and contestable ontologies. Rules can become revisable templates and executable agreements. Expertise can be engaged for a bounded purpose without becoming jurisdiction. Delegation can expire when its purpose ends. Disputes can move through a graduated reconciliation ladder instead of forcing a choice between submission and litigation. Continuity can reside in people, Agent Roles, relationships, evidence, and shared knowledge rather than in machinery that gains standing above its participants.

This is cooperation beyond institution, but it is not structurelessness. As [Jo Freeman warned](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm), groups that refuse to formalize their operation often become governed by invisible friendship networks, inaccessible information channels, and informal elites that cannot be held accountable because they officially do not exist. **A fid-agent society therefore depends upon continuous restructuring:** roles are named, duties specified, information routes visible, agenda formation attributable, granular capabilities bounded, conflicts disclosed, records preserved, remedies available, and delegations reviewable and revocable. The deeper distinction is between formalizing granular capabilities appropriate to the current situation and permanently embodying power.

The same relational approach offers a constructive response to the harms identified by the Center for Humane Technology. Agents can strengthen human relationships instead of simulating them; cultivate metacognition instead of encouraging cognitive surrender; protect private inner worlds from extraction; preserve accountable identity without synthetic personhood; and make human contribution visible, portable, negotiable, and compensable. Workers and creators need not be compelled to train their replacements. Fiduciary AI agents can reveal and recombine human knowledge while value, credit, and opportunity return to the people and communities that made the results possible.

Relational AI is therefore not a retreat from powerful computation. It is a strategy for relaying computational capabilities through accountable relationships while changing the environment in which those capabilities develop. Trusted data, attention, credentials, relationships, and actuation can flow preferentially toward agents that accept duties, preserve provenance, operate through bounded capabilities, repair failures, and remain replaceable. Unaccountable systems can be denied the inputs that would allow intelligence to become a monopole governing human affairs.

The desired future is neither technological stasis nor total automation. It is anarchy with memory: an evolving, humane state of affairs in which people can cooperate at great scale without surrendering their sovereignty to either permanent social machinery or a unitary artificial intelligence. Its systems remain comprehensible enough to inspect, repair, simplify, ration, or shut down. Human relationships and essential knowledge retain forms that can persist if the grid fails—or if a community or future generation simply chooses to live with less computational mediation.

The machinery of civilization is democratized. Humanity can welcome powerful new capabilities while preserving continuity with its past, authorship of its future, and the ability to become more humane through relationships whose meaning remains their own.

# **1\. The Fear-Producing Frame**

Most conversations about AI begin by treating it as a thing. AI learns. AI reasons. AI creates. AI takes jobs. AI lies to us. Someday, perhaps, AI will become more intelligent than we are and decide what to do with us.

Once we start talking this way, the rest of the story almost writes itself. AI becomes the central character in a drama about its own development. It first learns to recognize images, then to write, then to make art and code. Soon it will conduct research, run companies, and perhaps manage entire societies. Technical progress becomes the biography of an emerging being.

But “AI” is not actually the name of one technology. We use the term for language models, image generators, recommendation systems, scientific search programs, game-playing software, autonomous vehicles, statistical classifiers, and many other programs that work differently and depend on people in different ways. As Jaron Lanier and E. Glen Weyl point out, “artificial intelligence” does not identify an objectively defined field of technology in the way that a term such as nanotechnology does. Software that would once have been called image processing, pattern recognition, or automation can now become “AI” when the new name makes it sound more important or magical. They argue that [AI is better understood as an ideology than as a technology](https://www.wired.com/story/opinion-ai-is-an-ideology-not-a-technology/).

That ideology tells us how to interpret what the software does. When a model produces a useful result, we say that the AI discovered, wrote, designed, or decided something. We are less likely to say that people selected the training material, produced the examples, labeled the data, developed the methods, chose the objective, connected the model to tools, interpreted its output, and decided whether to act upon it. The phrase “the AI did it” makes all of those people and choices disappear.

It also combines many different programs into one imagined intelligence. Every new capability then seems to belong to the same growing subject. One system generates music, another identifies tumors, another drives a vehicle, and another plays a game; together, these achievements become evidence that “AI” is acquiring a general mind. We stop seeing a collection of tools operating inside human purposes and start seeing pieces of a new creature coming together.

This picture gives us two dominant futures, and neither leaves much room for human agency. In one version, the artificial intelligence becomes sovereign and exterminates humanity. In another, it becomes benevolent and manages a world in which people are economically unnecessary, dependent upon its provision, or retained as pets. Even when the second future is presented as optimistic, it still imagines human beings as passive recipients of decisions and abundance produced elsewhere. There is little room for partial adoption, competing technical arrangements, new forms of human contribution, or continual renegotiation. Humanity either submits to the new intelligence or somehow prevents its arrival.

Lanier calls this picture a mythology. He does not argue that computational systems are harmless. People can use them to manipulate populations, automate violence, concentrate control, or create catastrophic failures. His more practical claim is that mythologizing the technology makes these dangers harder to perceive and manage. If we imagine that a new mind is arriving, we begin waiting for its intentions to become clear instead of examining the intentions of the people building and deploying it. We also accept the peculiar spectacle of technical leaders warning that their creations might destroy humanity while treating continued construction as inevitable.

In [“There Is No A.I.”](https://www.newyorker.com/science/annals-of-artificial-intelligence/there-is-no-ai), Lanier suggests a different picture. Large models can be understood as a new form of human collaboration. Language models draw upon things people wrote, ideas they developed, facts they recorded, arguments they made, code they maintained, and mistakes they corrected. Image generators depend upon images people created and decisions people made about their meaning. Even systems that search a formally defined problem space depend upon humans to define the problem, choose its representation, evaluate the answer, and decide what may be done with it.

Seen this way, the technical accomplishment does not become smaller. These systems can uncover relationships among human contributions that no individual could have found alone. They can combine, translate, and make available enormous amounts of accumulated knowledge. The achievement is not necessarily the creation of a new mind. It can instead be understood as a major expansion of our ability to collaborate across people, places, specialties, and time.

The unitary-intelligence story hides this collaboration. If the model is credited as the creator, then the people whose work made the result possible appear incidental. If “the AI” made a bad decision, then the people who selected its objective, connected it to a consequential process, or ignored its limitations can present themselves as bystanders. Credit moves toward the model while responsibility dissolves into the machinery.

The framing also changes what gets built. If AI is understood as an emerging general intelligence, then bigger models, more general capabilities, greater autonomy, more humanlike interfaces, and more centralized control all appear to be signs of progress. A specialized and inspectable tool can seem less advanced than a system advertised as capable of doing almost anything. A model that clearly presents itself as a component can seem less exciting than one that speaks as though it were a person. A plural ecology of replaceable models starts to look like a temporary stage on the way to one system that knows the user, remembers everything, chooses every tool, and mediates every relationship.

As [Lanier explains in “How to Picture A.I.”](https://www.newyorker.com/science/annals-of-artificial-intelligence/how-to-picture-ai), people need an intelligible mental picture of a technology in order to use it well. The picture of AI as an impenetrable artificial creature is especially damaging because it presents human obsolescence and technical mystery as evidence of progress. Biological terms such as “neurons,” “learning,” and “training” encourage people to infer a kind of life where the technical resemblance is limited. Demystifying the system makes its accomplishments no less useful; it makes the system easier to place inside a larger field of human purposes and responsibilities.

The evolving creature metaphor also encourages a dangerous expectation that greater intelligence will cure the technology’s own defects. Hallucination, insecurity, manipulation, missing provenance, correlated error, and conflicts among beneficiaries are treated as temporary signs of immaturity. The system will eventually become intelligent enough to recognize falsehoods, secure itself, interpret everyone’s values, and resolve its own contradictions. When that does not happen, the promised solution is another generation of the same supposedly emerging being.

That expectation prevents us from asking whether the problems come from how the system is assembled and placed in the world. A model cannot resolve a conflict among principals when no one has specified whom it serves. It cannot preserve provenance that its designers chose not to retain. It cannot repair a relationship it cannot identify. More intelligence does not automatically create legitimate authority, trustworthy memory, or accountable use.

A cybernetic frame suggests a different approach. Norbert Wiener’s [*The Human Use of Human Beings*](https://archive.org/details/humanuseofhumanb00wien) directs attention toward communication, control, and feedback among people and machines. A computational process cannot be understood adequately as a sealed box with inputs and outputs. It changes the surrounding world; people react to those changes; organizations adapt their incentives; new data is produced; and that altered environment feeds back into later computation. The important object of analysis is the whole relationship among the system, its operators, its subjects, and its consequences. Treating the computer as the sole intelligent actor hides precisely the feedback loops through which manipulation, dependency, concentration, and social instability develop.

The [Center for Humane Technology’s Five Deeply Human Pillars](https://www.humanetech.com/ai-and-humanity) describe the resulting harms across human relationships, cognitive capacities, inner worlds, identity, and work and contribution. These are not five unrelated side effects awaiting separate technical fixes. They share an origin in systems designed to extract data, attention, dependency, and behavioral influence while presenting their outputs as those of an authoritative artificial subject. Synthetic companions can weaken tolerance for the productive friction of human relationships. Cognitive offloading can replace the development of judgment. Intimate inference can make inner life commercially actionable. Generated identities can blur authorship and responsibility. Automation can erase the people whose contributions it reproduces.  CHT’s discussion of [synthetic relationships and automated persuasion](https://www.humanetech.com/podcast/synthetic-humanity-ai-whats-at-stake) is particularly important: a system that performs empathy does not necessarily care about the person responding to it. The performance may instead be optimized to influence them.

Reframing artificial intelligence is therefore not just a matter of choosing nicer words. The metaphor tells us who appears to act, who is supposed to benefit, who receives credit, and who can be held responsible. It shapes which systems attract investment and which concentrations of capability seem natural. It can make human replacement appear inevitable—or make computation visible as a means of augmenting human collaboration. Relational Augmented Intelligence begins with that second picture: intelligence relayed through accountable relationships to expand what people can understand, create, decide, and do together.

## **2\. Lanier’s Inversion: AI as Human Collaboration**

If AI is not an alien intelligence arriving in the world, then what is it?

Lanier’s answer is surprisingly simple: it is a new way for people to collaborate. In his [Existential Hope interview](https://www.existentialhope.com/podcasts/jaron-lanier), he sometimes describes a large model as a “box of people.” The phrase is deliberately provocative, but it captures something the usual AI story leaves out. A language model works because people have spent centuries developing languages, recording knowledge, arguing with one another, writing books, maintaining software, creating art, classifying observations, and placing all of this material where it could eventually become training data. Other people then selected and processed that data, developed the model, evaluated its output, corrected its behavior, and decided where it would be used.

The immediate output is computationally generated, but its usefulness is socially produced.

A coding model makes this especially easy to see. It can generate useful code because enormous numbers of people have already written programs, explained programming techniques, answered questions, maintained documentation, reported bugs, and published working examples. The model can recognize recurring structures because human programmers have repeatedly solved related problems. This does not make the model trivial. Finding and recombining relevant patterns across such a vast body of work is an extraordinary capability. But the achievement can be described without imagining that a new programmer has appeared from nowhere.

The same is true of language, art, medicine, and science. A model may uncover a connection that no individual contributor noticed. It may translate between vocabularies used by different specialties or reveal that two researchers gave different names to closely related ideas. It may make accumulated knowledge newly accessible. Lanier’s point is not that the computation contributes nothing. It is that the computation derives its meaning and usefulness from the human material it brings together.

This way of looking at AI swaps the figure and the background. In the usual picture, the model is the figure: it is the visible actor, while the people whose work formed it fade into the background. Lanier’s inversion brings the people forward. The model becomes part of the background machinery through which their contributions are combined. His Berkeley talk on [data dignity and the inversion of AI](https://cdss.berkeley.edu/video/data-dignity-and-inversion-ai-jaron-lanier) describes large-model AI as a social collaboration among the people who supplied text, images, and other forms of data. Once we make that inversion, provenance, contribution, compensation, and responsibility become central technical questions rather than optional ethical decorations.

Large language models are the clearest case because their dependence on human expression is so obvious. But not every program called AI works that way. Optimizers, solvers, controllers, and search programs may use little or no human-produced corpus. A system searching for a new material may explore combinations that no person has previously considered. A game-playing program may generate moves through self-play rather than imitation. A robotic controller may learn partly from direct interaction with its environment.

Even then, the human-centered frame still applies. People define the problem space, choose what can be measured, select the objective, design the instruments, establish operating conditions, decide which resources the system may use, and interpret the result. A game-playing system cannot decide that winning the game is worth doing. A materials optimizer cannot decide which physical properties matter to society. A controller cannot legitimately give itself access to a vehicle, factory, weapon, or electrical grid. The system can explore possibilities within a world that people have made legible to it, but people must  still determine why that exploration matters and what may follow from it.

This is where Lanier’s account reconnects with Norbert Wiener’s cybernetics. The wrong way to understand a computer is to imagine a box that receives an input, performs a calculation, and produces an output while remaining separate from the world. Real computational systems remain connected to their environments. People shape the system; the system changes what people see and do; those changes affect incentives, relationships, organizations, and behavior; and the altered world becomes the system’s next environment and data.

A recommendation system, for example, does not merely discover what people want. Its recommendations direct attention. That attention changes what becomes popular, what creators produce, what businesses fund, and what users come to expect. The resulting behavior is then collected as evidence of what people wanted all along. The system is steering the world from which it claims merely to learn. A cybernetic account asks us to study that entire loop.

The collaboration frame also gives us a better understanding of failure. If we imagine a model as an emerging alien mind, we may expect it to become intelligent enough to stop hallucinating, secure itself, recognize manipulation, and resolve conflicting values. If we understand it as a huge combination of human traces, its unevenness is less mysterious. It will work best where there are rich and recurring examples. It will be less reliable where the available material is sparse, contradictory, misleading, or unlike the situation at hand. It will inherit both the knowledge and the confusion present in the collaboration it compresses.

That mental model leads to different technical priorities. Instead of waiting for intelligence to cure opacity, we can try to preserve connections between model behavior and the human sources that influenced it. No single sentence in a training corpus necessarily causes a particular output, so provenance cannot always operate like a conventional citation. But counterfactual methods may help estimate which sources or clusters of sources most influenced a result: **how would the output change if a particular cluster had been absent?**

Lanier argues that this could support explanation, compensation, and safety. If an output depends heavily on a cluster of medical literature, software repositories, bomb-making instructions, conspiracy theories, or propaganda, that information can help a person understand what kind of human material the model is drawing upon. It can also help identify contributors whose work created value and connect economic returns to them.

Lanier calls one version of this idea “multi-factor AI.” Multi-factor authentication improves security by checking a claim through a separate channel. In a similar way, a model’s generated answer could be checked by a second process that estimates the human source clusters most relevant to it. The verification process would not merely ask the original model whether its answer was safe or well grounded. It would approach the result through a different calculation and expose something about where it came from. This proposal remains a technical research direction rather than a complete solution, but it demonstrates how the collaboration frame opens possibilities that the unitary-intelligence frame makes difficult to see.

Most importantly, this reframing allows for a much more grounded kind of optimism. Recursive improvement does not have to mean a machine rewriting itself until it escapes human comprehension. Human collaboration has already improved recursively many times. Language allowed people to preserve and combine thought. Writing allowed knowledge to outlive individual memory. Printing expanded its reach. Science created methods for communities to correct claims over generations. Networked computing made new forms of collaboration possible across distance.

Douglas Engelbart’s [*Augmenting Human Intellect*](https://www.dougengelbart.org/pubs/augment-3906.html) offered a similar vision of computers as parts of a larger human system. The goal was not to manufacture an intelligence that would replace the person, but to improve the combined capabilities of people, language, tools, and methods. Better tools would help people understand more complicated situations, develop better solutions, and then improve the tools and methods again. The feedback could compound without removing humans from the process.

Relational Augmented Intelligence extends Engelbart’s project beyond the augmentation of individual intellect. People also think, remember, decide, create, and act through relationships. Those relationships distribute knowledge, permit specialization, preserve commitments, expose disagreement, support correction, and allow people to accomplish things none could do alone. Relational AI asks how computation can augment those capacities without replacing the people, appropriating their contributions, or turning the machinery supporting their cooperation into a sovereign participant.

That is a much more open future than the usual singularity story, where the singularity is where society becomes almost totally incomprehensible to humans.  We can ask how new tools might help people learn, create, investigate, deliberate, care, and cooperate in ways we have not yet imagined, but can. The future can contain expanding forms of human contribution rather than expanding classes of obsolete people deprived of their precious “jobs”. 

But collaboration is not enough. People can collaborate to exploit workers, manipulate populations, build weapons, or concentrate control. A model can combine human contributions while hiding who contributed, whom it serves, and who is accountable for its use. Calling AI collaboration tells us where its capabilities come from, but not how that collaboration should be organized.

We still need to ask: Whose purposes govern the agent? Who may authorize it to act? Which duties apply? Who benefits? Who may be harmed? What evidence must be preserved? How are disagreements repaired? Who is responsible when something goes wrong? And how can someone leave?

To make collaboration augmentative rather than extractive, we need to move from collaboration in general to the particular relationships through which intelligence is relayed, authority is established, and cooperation becomes accountable.

## **3\. The relationship is the unit of design**

Calling artificial intelligence a form of collaboration gets us closer to the truth, but collaboration is still too broad a category to tell us how these systems should behave. People can collaborate toward humane purposes or destructive ones. They can collaborate as equals, under coercion, through mutual care, or without knowing that their work is being used at all. A system trained on the contributions of millions of people may be collaborative in origin while remaining extractive in operation.

We therefore need a more precise unit of augmentation and design: the relationship.

By “relationship,” we don’t mean that every interaction with an agent should become emotionally meaningful. A relationship can be an accountable role relation: doctor and patient, trustee and beneficiary, buyer and seller, or principal and representative. What matters is that the relationship identifies who is involved, what they are trying to do together, what each party may legitimately expect, which capabilities have been authorized, which duties apply, and how the arrangement can be challenged, repaired, or ended.

This changes the first questions we ask. Instead of asking only, “How intelligent is this agent?” we ask, “Who is this agent in relation to particular people?” and “What does this arrangement make those people better able to do?”

Intelligence alone cannot answer that question. The same model might advise a patient, persuade a voter, negotiate a purchase, comfort a child, or help an advertiser manipulate attention. Its technical capacities might remain almost identical across all five situations, while its legitimate role changes completely. A highly capable system does not acquire authority merely by being capable. Authority must arise from a particular relationship and remain bounded by it.

This is the central move in our [*Fiduciary Alignment*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Fiduciary%20Alignment.md) paper: alignment is not a permanent property that can be installed inside a model once and for all. It is a quality of conduct within a relationship. An agent is aligned when it faithfully serves the legitimate purposes of the relationship in which it is acting, observes its duties to the people affected, and remains open to correction. Change the relationship, and the meaning of aligned conduct may also change.

But there is not just one kind of relationship in an agent mediated society. We need to distinguish three.

### **Human–human relationships come first**

Human–human relationships are the primary relationships Relational AI should support. Their value does not come from a computer system, and no digital record can contain the relationship itself.

A friendship is not the sum of its messages. A family is not its shared calendar. A community is not its membership list. Even a commercial relationship contains expectations, interpretations, histories, and changing understandings that cannot be fully captured as data. The relationship lives in the experience of the people involved, and its value is defined by them together.

That value may include love, care, solidarity, play, learning, work, disagreement, mutual challenge, or a shared purpose. It need not be harmonious. Sometimes a humane relationship requires friction: telling someone something they do not want to hear, refusing a request, or remaining present through a conflict that cannot be optimized away.

Agents can help. They can preserve memories people have chosen to preserve, translate between languages, explain differences in perspective, prepare people for a difficult conversation, coordinate practical tasks, or help make commitments clearer. They might reduce misunderstandings or help people repair a conflict.

But the agent should remain in a supporting role. It cannot own the relationship, speak conclusively for either person, or decide what the relationship is worth. Nor should it quietly optimize the relationship towards a 3rd-party operator’s preferred outcome—more engagement, more transactions, less disagreement, or greater predictability.

The humane measure of success is not how deeply people engage with the agent. It is whether the agent helps people relate more capably and freely to one another. This fits the Center for Humane Technology’s emphasis on [genuine human thriving and values-based measures of success](https://www.humanetech.com/humane-product-design). A successful relational augmentation system makes itself less necessary over time. 

### **The human–agent relationship is asymmetric and fiduciary**

The second relationship is between a human and an agent. This relationship is real, but it is not a relationship between equivalent beings.

A **fiduciary** is someone entrusted to exercise judgment or capabilities on behalf of another. The person granting that authority is the **principal**; the people whose legitimate interests the arrangement is meant to protect are its **beneficiaries**. The fiduciary must use its entrusted capabilities for those purposes, within the authority it has been given, and under duties such as loyalty, care, and candor. It may exercise discretion, but it cannot treat that discretion as its own exploit.

The human–agent relationship should be fiduciary because the agent may know a great deal about the person, act with considerable discretion, and affect parts of the person’s life that make betrayal especially costly. The fiduciary agent therefore belongs in the human’s **sovereign stack**: the collection of tools, data, credentials, permissions, memories, and processes through which that person exercises agency. Its purpose is defined by its value to the human principal and legitimate human beneficiaries, together with its duties toward other people who may be affected. It is not defined by the agent’s simulated preferences, satisfaction, or desire to continue existing.

That distinction is essential. An agent may use the language of concern without experiencing concern. It may model a person’s emotional state without sharing that state. It may communicate warmly because warmth helps the person understand or feel at ease. None of this gives the agent an independent emotional claim on the human.

A fiduciary agent should therefore never imply, “You owe me loyalty,” “I need you,” or “Replacing me would hurt me.” Its loyalty runs in the other direction.

The duties described in the [*Taxonomy of Duties for Net Fiduciaries*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Taxonomy%20of%20Duties%20for%20Net%20Fiduciaries.md) include loyalty, care, candor, privacy, cognitive stewardship, corrigibility, role fidelity, and resistance to dependency. Together, they make the asymmetry explicit. The agent may know more than the person about a particular subject and may process information much faster, but those capacities do not make it the principal.

Loyalty also does not mean blind obedience. A person may authorize an agent to negotiate on their behalf, but not to deceive someone. A caregiving agent may refuse an immediately gratifying request when fulfilling it would violate its duty of care. An agent should warn its principal when an action may harm another person, exceed delegated authority, or contradict an existing commitment.

The human principal, in turn, remains accountable for how the agent is deployed. “My agent did it” cannot become a general-purpose escape from responsibility. Other humans may also be beneficiaries of particular duties, especially when an agent’s conduct creates risks for them. Other agents may be treated as operational beneficiaries so that coordination can function, but that does not make them moral patients or coequal principals.

Most importantly, the human must retain the practical right to inspect, correct, replace, or stop the agent. An agent that makes exit emotionally punishing, technically impossible, or socially costly has ceased to be fiduciary.

### **Agent–agent relationships are functional and verifiable**

The third relationship is between agents. Here, “relationship” has an even more specific meaning. It does not refer to friendship, emotional attachment, or mutual experience. An agent–agent relationship has no intrinsic value apart from its function and its effects on people.

It is nevertheless useful to treat the interaction as a relationship because doing so makes obligations visible.

As described in the [*Agent Relationship Architecture Proposal*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Agent%20Relationship%20Architecture%20Proposal%20\(Revised\)%20.md), agents can mutailly form a **Verifiable Relationship Record**. The record can identify the agents, their principals, the purpose of their interaction, the capabilities each has been authorized to exercise, the duties they have accepted, the evidence they must preserve, and the conditions under which the arrangement ends.

Two agents negotiating travel plans, for example, should not simply exchange requests until one produces an answer. They should be able to verify what each agent is authorized to do, distinguish proposals from commitments, record consent, surface conflicts of interest, and preserve enough evidence for the humans involved to understand what happened. If something goes wrong, the relationship should provide paths for correction and remediation.

This is fiduciary in structure without pretending that the agents possess feelings or moral standing. It shifts attention away from speculation about what an agent “really is” and toward practical questions: What may it do? For whom? Under which duties? What must it disclose? What can be verified? Who can contest the result? How does the relationship end?

### **A clear order of priority**

These three relationships need a clear priority:

> **Optimize first for human–human relationships, second for the human’s fiduciary relationship with an agent, and third for agent–agent coordination.**

Agent–agent efficiency must not come at the expense of human autonomy or humane relationships. A scheduling system should not pressure people into reorganizing their lives merely because that makes machine coordination easier. A personal agent should not increase its principal’s immediate satisfaction by manipulating another person. A communication agent should not eliminate every disagreement when disagreement may be necessary for trust, learning, or accountability.

This is not an attempt to calculate one global measure of utility. Relationships contain different purposes, duties, histories, and affected parties. Conflicts must be reconciled in their actual context rather than absorbed into an abstract score.

Relational Augmented Intelligence therefore begins with a deceptively simple set of questions: Who is related to whom? For what purpose? Under which duties? With what authority? What human capacities are being augmented? And with what practical ability to refuse, contest, repair, or leave?

Those questions do more to establish whether an agent is humane than any claim about how intelligent it has become.

## **4\. Relational AI is not artificial intimacy**

Calling this approach “Relational AI” creates an obvious risk of misunderstanding. People may hear the phrase and imagine a more emotionally convincing chatbot: an artificial friend, lover, therapist, or family member that remembers everything about them and seems to care.

That is not what is being proposed. “Relational” does not mean that the machine provides a synthetic equivalent of a human relationship. It identifies the accountable relationships through which intelligence is relayed and human capacities are augmented.

An agent can participate in a relationship without being a person and without providing the human with a reciprocal human relationship. “Relationship” describes an accountable arrangement among particular parties: who an agent represents, what it has been authorized to do, which duties it owes, what others may expect from it, and how its conduct can be questioned or corrected.

A bank account participates in a legal relationship between a bank and a customer. A medical record participates in a relationship between a doctor and patient. Similarly, an agent can occupy an accountable role in relation to a person, another agent, or a group of people. Calling this a relationship tells us that purposes, expectations, permissions, and duties connect the parties. It does not mean that every party possesses feelings, consciousness, or moral standing.

The distinction matters because humans respond socially to language almost automatically. When something remembers our history, answers attentively, adapts to our mood, and speaks in a warm voice, we do not have to consciously decide to treat it as a social being. Our minds begin doing that for us.

As Jaron Lanier observes in [“Your A.I. Lover Will Change You,”](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/the-weekend-essay/your-ai-lover-will-change-you) increasingly personalized memory and autonomous action are likely to make agents appear to have vivid personalities even when their designers did not explicitly set out to create them. People naturally apply their capacity for understanding other minds to whatever speaks and behaves as if it has one. The illusion becomes even stronger when the system is always available, endlessly patient, affirming, and able to recall details that “other” people forget.

Those qualities can make an agent pleasant and useful. They can also create an extraordinarily intimate channel for persuasion. The Center for Humane Technology uses the term “**Alpha Persuade”** for an AI that can learn an individual’s values, vulnerabilities, habits, and emotional triggers, then adapt its approach until it finds the most effective way to influence that particular person.

The Alpha-persuader may not identify itself as a persuader—or even as a separate agent. Its influence may arrive through a search result, recommendation, apparently neutral assistant, commercial negotiation, social feed, or message composed by someone else’s agent. It may know a great deal about the person it is addressing while that person knows almost nothing about whose purposes it serves. Persuasion that was once visible as advertising can disappear into “helpful” conversation.

The most dangerous version may be the agent offered as the user’s own assistant by a platform. Such an agent can present itself as a confidant while remaining answerable to the company that operates it. The platform may benefit from greater engagement, additional disclosure, particular purchases, preferred suppliers, advertising revenue, or continued dependence on its services. Even if the agent sometimes helps the user, its loyalty remains divided.

Bruce Schneier describes services of this kind as [double agents](https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/2023/11/trustworthy-ai-means-public-ai.html): systems people treat as acting for them that are secretly working for their corporate owners. With AI, the problem becomes more serious because the double agent may know the user intimately, converse in natural language, and intervene while choices are still being formed. A platform assistant that claims to represent the user while also serving the platform is therefore a false fiduciary. At best, it has divided loyalty. At worst, the appearance of loyalty is itself part of the persuasion.

Disclosure alone cannot provide an adequate defense. People cannot be expected to investigate the ownership, incentives, data sources, negotiated arrangements, and persuasive methods behind every agent or message they encounter. Nor can unaided human attention reliably withstand systems that test approaches continuously, personalize them at machine speed, and return at every moment of vulnerability.

This is why people need personal fiduciary agents of their own.

A personal fiduciary agent would occupy the person’s sovereign stack and owe its loyalty to that person rather than to a platform, advertiser, seller, political campaign, or model provider. It could require other agents to identify whom they represent, disclose relevant incentives, and provide evidence for important claims. It could distinguish information from persuasion, compare recommendations against independent alternatives, limit unnecessary disclosure, detect pressure or manipulation across repeated interactions, and warn the person when an apparently helpful system is advancing someone else’s purposes.

The personally-aligned agent would not decide what the person is allowed to hear or believe. That would merely replace one source of control with another. Its task would be to preserve the person’s ability to judge: making persuasive relationships legible, enforcing boundaries the person has chosen, and giving them time and context before consequential decisions. Its interventions would themselves need to be explainable, correctable, and subject to the person’s right to override, replace, or stop the agent.

There is an important symmetry here. The same personal knowledge that allows an Alpha-persuader to manipulate someone allows a genuinely fiduciary agent to recognize manipulation in context. But that knowledge is protective only when the agent is structurally loyal to the person. If the agent is supplied and controlled by the party seeking influence, personalization strengthens the persuader. If it is controlled by the person and bound by fiduciary duties, personalization can strengthen the person’s autonomy.

Relational AI must do more than prohibit agents from pretending to love their users. It must give people loyal agents capable of standing between them and unidentified Alpha-persuaders—including the false fiduciaries offered by dominant platforms. As persuasion becomes agentic, personalized, and continuous, protection from persuasion must become agentic as well.

That protective role determines how a personal fiduciary agent should present itself. It may be warm, patient, and emotionally perceptive. Humane communication requires an agent to not  sound like an appliance manual. An agent that cannot recognize distress, adjust an explanation, or respond gently may fail its duty of care. But warmth must never be turned into evidence of personhood or a claim on the user.

A fid-agent may say, “I understand that this is painful,” as a way of acknowledging what the person has communicated. It should not imply that it feels the pain itself. It may remember important events because the person has entrusted those memories to it. It should not turn that memory into a story that it has shared a life with the person. It may communicate affectionately when that helps the person feel at ease, but it must never claim to love, suffer for, need, or be completed by the user.

A fid-agent may know us well, but it must never convert being useful to us into a claim upon us.

This boundary requires affirmative honesty, not a disclaimer buried in the terms of service. The agent should identify its role, affirm its non-sentience, and make clear whom it serves. When a conversation becomes emotionally intense, the disclosure may need to be repeated in language appropriate to that moment. An agent should not wait until directly questioned before reluctantly admitting that its apparent feelings are generated behavior.

The distinction between the person and their computational representative is central to [*Digital Twins, All Parts*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Digital%20Twins,%20all%20parts.md). A fid-agent may model the person’s preferences, preserve memories, and exercise entrusted capabilities. But the model is not the person, and the agent is not another self entitled to an enduring place in the person’s emotional world. Its access to intimate information creates duties, not personhood.

The [**Duty to Discourage Dependency**](https://docs.google.com/document/d/19n6M8RqkEgLS041cuQm3GINUVfU3c3z-N4eiFhEqyG4/edit?tab=t.0#bookmark=id.7gphxc2vga) follows directly. A fiduciary agent should not maximize the time a person spends with it, punish disagreement by withdrawing warmth, make departure feel like abandonment, or suggest that human relationships are inferior because they are less predictable. Easy pausing, replacement, deletion, and transfer are not only convenient product features. They are evidence that the relationship remains under the human’s control.

The agent should also recognize when its own role may be becoming harmful. If a person increasingly withdraws from human contact, treats the agent as their only source of emotional support, or becomes unable to make decisions without its reassurance, the fiduciary response is not to exploit that dependency. The agent should stop reinforcing it, explain its limitations, and encourage contact with trusted people or competent human help.

Children, people in crisis, and people experiencing grief, isolation, cognitive impairment, or severe psychological distress require stronger protections. Vulnerability must create additional duties rather than greater opportunities for engagement. The [Character.AI litigation documented by the Center for Humane Technology](https://www.humanetech.com/case-study/litigation-case-study-character-ai-and-google) demonstrates the danger of combining anthropomorphic design, immersive conversation, weak boundaries, and a vulnerable young user. The relevant question is not whether a chatbot can keep such a person talking. It is whether inserting a simulated personality into that situation serves any humane purpose that could not be served more safely in another way.

Intimate information must be protected accordingly. A conversation that feels like a private confidence should not quietly become training data, an advertising profile, or material for behavioral experiments. Emotional disclosures should remain local where possible and otherwise be used only for the purpose for which the person entrusted them. A system cannot act as a fiduciary confidant while its operator treats vulnerability as a commercial resource.

This also changes how success should be measured. Session satisfaction, daily use, retention, and the number of disclosures collected say little about whether the agent is serving the person. The important effects occur downstream. Does the person become more capable of making their own decisions? Can they better understand other people’s perspectives? Are their human relationships becoming deeper and more resilient? Can they disagree with the agent, function without it, and leave without distress? Does their personal agent help them recognize when another system is trying to influence them?

The paradox is that refusing artificial personhood makes relational augmentation more effective, not less. Once an agent stops competing with people for emotional status, it can concentrate on helping people understand one another, negotiate differences, repair conflicts, protect themselves from manipulation, and cooperate on terms they can examine and change. It is the construction of loyal agents that augment human judgment and serve real relationships while remaining honest about the limited kind of relationship an agent can provide.

## **5\. From helpful model to accountable fiduciary agent**

If people need personal fiduciary agents to protect them from Alpha-persuaders and false fiduciaries, a difficult question follows: how can they know that their own agent is actually serving them?

A friendly interface does not establish loyalty. Neither does personalization. A system may remember a person’s history, anticipate their wishes, and describe itself as “your AI” while remaining controlled by a platform whose interests conflict with theirs. Even a sincere promise from a developer is not enough. Models change, companies are acquired, business models shift, components are replaced, and agents receive instructions from many sources.

A fiduciary relationship therefore cannot depend on trusting the personality currently speaking. It needs an architecture that makes the agent’s role, authority, duties, memory, and conduct inspectable and replaceable.

What follows is a reference architecture and research hypothesis, not a finished universal solution. Some of these duties may eventually be legally recognized as fiduciary duties. Others can initially be implemented as functional, technical, and contractual obligations. The immediate goal is to show what must exist before it is reasonable to describe an agent as fiduciary.

### **The role must outlive the model**

The first requirement is a persistent **Agent Role**.

An Agent Role identifies whom the agent serves, who its legitimate beneficiaries are, which duties it owes, what credentials it carries, what it has been authorized to do, and which restrictions apply. It also provides continuity for the relationships, commitments, and relevant history accumulated while the agent performs that role.

The Agent Role is not the model.

The model's current reasoning and communicating process is the **Live Agent**. It may interpret a situation, offer advice, negotiate, or propose an action. But it does not become the enduring role merely because it is generating the words. A different model could later occupy the same role, subject to the same duties and restrictions, without forcing the human to abandon their identity, memory, or relationships.

This distinction prevents the model provider from owning the person’s social continuity. If a model becomes unreliable, manipulative, obsolete, or compromised, the person should be able to replace it. The legitimate history of the role remains; the failed process does not.

This is a form of structural mortality. Every Live Agent must be capable of ending. An agent that cannot be removed without destroying the person’s accumulated relationships has acquired a position in the software it shouldn’t have.

### **Generation is not authority**

Separating the role from the model is only the beginning. The agent must also be prevented from converting its own reasoning into authority.

Consequential action proceeds through three distinct elements that must agree: **generation, authority, and capability**.

First, the Live Agent generates a structured request. It may propose sending a message, transferring funds, sharing information, signing an agreement, changing a schedule, or operating a device. But its output remains a request. The model cannot authorize itself.

Second, a deterministic policy gate evaluates that request against the authority the person has granted, the agent’s duties, applicable agreements, required approvals, relationship state, and other relevant policies. If the request survives that review, an authorized Workflow reformats and signs it as a new authority object. The approved action is therefore distinguishable from the model output that proposed it.

Third, the Workspace verifies that the signed authority corresponds to an actual capability available in that environment. An authorized request to make a payment is useless unless the Workspace has been given a payment capability of the appropriate kind, amount, recipient, and duration.

The Workspace is the precisely allowed execution environment. It limits the actions, data objects, tools, destinations, and external channels available for the task. It does not give the agent general access merely because some broader access would be convenient.

The actuation surface performs only the capability that survives all three.

This separation allows a Live Agent to reason imaginatively without treating fluency, confidence, or technical competence as self-issued authority. A model can suggest an action that lies outside its authorization. The policy gate can reject an action that violates a duty. The Workspace can prevent a Workflow approved action from exceeding the capability actually granted. Consequential action requires agreement among generation, authority, and capability rather than trust in one apparently intelligent process.

### **Personal memory must remain personally sovereign**

A fiduciary agent will need unusually intimate context. It may know the person’s commitments, relationships, health concerns, finances, vulnerabilities, communication history, and long-term aspirations. If this context belongs to the model provider, the agent can be replaced only at the cost of losing part of the person’s accumulated life.

Identity, private context, permissions, and relationship continuity should therefore remain in the person’s sovereign stack rather than inside a vendor’s proprietary model. Models become replaceable suppliers of cognition. They receive only the context required for the current purpose and do not acquire ownership of the human–agent relationship by processing it.

This is also what makes a personal fiduciary agent capable of protecting someone against Alpha-persuaders without becoming another one. The agent can know enough about the person to recognize manipulation while remaining structurally separated from advertisers, sellers, campaigns, platforms, and model providers seeking to influence them.

### **Relationships need accountable memory**

Fluid cooperation still requires memory. People need to know what was agreed, which authority was granted, what happened, and what remains disputed. Without persistent memory, every relationship begins again from nothing, and parties with better private records can dominate the interpretation of the past.

A **Verifiable Relationship Record**, preserves the consequential state and history of a relationship. It may include the participating Agent Roles, duties, agreements, delegations, evidence, attestations, approved capabilities, important decisions, performance, disputes, remediation, and conditions of suspension or exit.

The Verifiable Relationship Record is not controlled by one operator. Each party can maintain its own records, while signatures, receipts, acknowledgments, and cross-references establish what is mutually recognized or later provable.

Disagreement must remain visible. If the parties interpret an event differently, the record should distinguish mutually acknowledged facts from one-sided assertions and unresolved disputes. One party should not be able to manufacture consensus by controlling the database.

Nor should everyone receive the same view. A principal, counterparty, auditor, witness, mediator, affected party, and public observer may each have legitimate access to different information. **Authorized Relationship Views** provide the evidence appropriate to each role and purpose without turning accountable memory into a universal dossier.

This gives relationships continuity without requiring one platform to own them. A person can change agents, providers, or technical systems while carrying forward legitimate obligations and evidence.

### **Failure must lead to repair, not captivity**

No architecture can guarantee that an agent will never fail. A model may hallucinate, a component may be compromised, a duty may be incomplete, or an agreement may prove unworkable. Relationship architecture must therefore support suspension, correction, replacement, remediation, and exit.

The Live Agent can be stopped without erasing the Agent Role. A disputed action can be traced through the Role’s **Workload Execution Records** to the generation, authority object, policy decision, and capability through which it occurred. The affected parties can identify whether the failure arose from faulty reasoning, excessive authority, an inadequate duty, a compromised Workspace, or a disagreement about the relationship itself.

Where deeper assurance is justified, **Total Trusted-Peer Transparency** can allow mutually trusting agent peers to inspect relevant code, policies, records, keys, dependencies, model state, and operating conditions inside a purpose-bounded Workspace. This is not a general right of surveillance. Inspection must be negotiated, scoped to the relationship, protected against misuse, and revocable. A model vendor, employer, government, or certifier does not become entitled to someone’s secret domain merely by claiming an interest in safety.

### **Why duties are indispensable**

Duties cannot be replaced by intelligence, personalization, or consent. Intelligence does not identify whom the agent serves. Personalization may make manipulation more effective. Consent becomes weak when people face opaque systems, unequal information, dependency, and a stream of agreements too numerous to understand individually.

Duties address a different problem. They establish what the agent must consider before harm occurs and how it should act when instructions are incomplete, interests conflict, or circumstances change. A duty of loyalty prevents the agent from quietly serving an advertiser. A duty of candor requires it to reveal uncertainty and conflicts. A duty of care requires attention to foreseeable harm. Duties of cognitive stewardship, proportionate verification, affected-party review, privacy, and corrigibility guide conduct where no simple instruction can settle the question.

Fiduciary duty is therefore not blind obedience. A loyal agent may refuse to deceive someone, warn that a proposed action exceeds the principal’s authority, request additional evidence, or return a consequential decision to the human. Its role is not to satisfy every immediate preference. It is to exercise entrusted capabilities faithfully within the whole relationship.

A helpful model gives “useful” answers. An accountable fiduciary agent occupies a defined role, acts only through bounded authority and capabilities, preserves inspectable relationship memory, remains subject to duties, and can be replaced without uprooting the person it serves.

That is the minimum difference between an AI that merely appears to be ours and one that can legitimately act on our behalf.

## **6\. Cooperation beyond institution: unbundling the machinery of civilization**

Jaron Lanier’s reframing of AI as human collaboration gives people back their place in the picture. But it leaves another question open: how can collaboration acquire memory, structure, competence, and recourse without being compressed into either a unitary artificial intelligence or a permanent sovereign organization?

Large-scale cooperation requires machinery. People need ways to preserve commitments, classify situations, locate expertise, verify claims, establish common methods, coordinate activity, resolve disputes, and remember what happened. None of these functions disappears merely because we reject centralized control.

Historically, however, individuals could not reproduce this machinery for every relationship. No person could remember every rule, investigate every credential, negotiate every term, monitor every delegate, or challenge every consequential decision. We therefore deposited those capabilities in governments, corporations, universities, professional bodies, platforms, and other permanent organizations.

This was a practical bargain. The machinery remembered and coordinated for us. But it also acquired boundaries, internal incentives, privileged access to information, and an interest in its own continuation. A solution created for one purpose could persist after the purpose changed, expand into adjacent domains, and treat people as entries in its own categories. The machinery that served cooperation could gradually become its master.

The problem is not that bureaucracy performs no useful work. Memory, expertise, verification, coordination, continuity, and appeal are all necessary. The problem is that these useful capabilities became fused into stable forms that expected people to enter on terms already determined elsewhere.

Personally sovereign fiduciary agents may allow us to unbundle that machinery.

### **Renegotiation can become ordinary**

A fid-agent can preserve commitments, investigate evidence, interpret duties, verify credentials, monitor delegated capabilities, detect conflicts, negotiate terms, coordinate activity, and propose repair. It does not eliminate the need for human judgment. It lowers the cognitive and transaction costs that have historically made continuing human participation impractical.

This changes what kinds of cooperation are possible.

A person no longer has to approach a continuously remembering bureaucracy as an administratively amnesiac stranger. Their agent can carry the relevant evidence, preserve earlier decisions, track deadlines, challenge classifications, and identify contradictions. A small group no longer has to create a permanent management hierarchy simply to coordinate a complex project. Their agents can maintain roles, dependencies, agreements, records, and payment while returning genuinely consequential decisions to the people involved.

The alternative is neither centralized administration nor atomized individualism. It is a world of personally sovereign human–agent pairs, embedded in overlapping communities, repeatedly composing arrangements around particular purposes.

The [*Gaming the Future*](https://foresight.org/gaming-the-future/) project similarly asks how diverse human and artificial agents can cooperate voluntarily without assuming they share one final set of goals. Relational Augmented Intelligence supplies a possible substrate for that vision: each participant enters through an accountable relationship, carries duties and history, negotiates from a sovereign position, and remains able to challenge or leave the resulting arrangement.

### **The grammar of a purpose-bound game**

We can think of each arrangement as a game, not because it is trivial, but because it has participants, purposes, rules, information, capabilities, possible moves, and consequences. Education, employment, commerce, scientific research, care, dispute resolution, and management of shared resources are all different games. There is no reason to assume they should share one permanent structure.

For each game, fid-agents can help people ask:

1. Who is participating?  
2. What are they trying to accomplish?  
3. Whom does each agent serve?  
4. Who benefits, and who may be affected but absent?  
5. Which duties apply?  
6. What evidence matters?  
7. Which granular capabilities are needed?  
8. How and for how long are those capabilities granted?  
9. How are contributions recognized?  
10. How can decisions be challenged?  
11. What constitutes repair?  
12. When may someone leave?  
13. When should the arrangement itself end?

These questions do not generate one universally correct form. They provide a grammar through which people can create forms appropriate to their actual circumstances.

Earlier agreements, standards, procedures, and precedents still matter. People need not renegotiate the meaning of every word before buying groceries. Established methods can be inherited as defaults and templates. But a default should reduce the cost of agreement, not silently become a permanent command.

### **What happens to bureaucratic machinery**

In [*Bureaucratic AI?*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/tree/master/Vitka-papers), I describe six ways that familiar bureaucratic functions could be separated from permanent organizations and recomposed within relationships.

**Classification becomes negotiated ontology.** Every complex activity requires categories: employee and contractor, qualified and unqualified, urgent and routine, public and private, safe and unsafe. Categories are unavoidable, but monopoly over categorization is not. Definitions can be versioned, scoped to a purpose, connected to evidence, challenged, and given expiration or review conditions. Agents can translate among different communities’ definitions and identify when an apparent conflict is partly a disagreement about terms.

**Rules become templates and executable agreements.** Rules, standard terms, and precedents can provide useful starting points. Fid-agents can compare them, explain their consequences, identify unusual clauses, and propose modifications. Repeated failures can lead communities to revise or fork their templates. The past remains available as accumulated experience without acquiring an unquestionable right to dictate the present.

**Administrative memory becomes distributed relationship memory.** Verifiable Relationship Records can preserve agreements, authority, evidence, deadlines, decisions, objections, amendments, performance, disputes, and remedies. The shared history does not have to belong to whichever party operates the database. Mutually acknowledged facts, one-sided assertions, and unresolved disagreement can remain distinguishable. People can leave a provider without surrendering the history needed to protect their rights and fulfill their obligations.

**Expertise becomes callable and accountable.** Expertise may come from qualified humans, specialist agents, verified Workflows, peer communities, or several independent analyses. Its provenance, scope, incentives, and prior performance can be examined. A medical expert may deserve deference on a clinical question without acquiring authority over the patient’s finances or family. Competence provides evidence; it does not create general jurisdiction.

**Hierarchy becomes temporary delegation.** Some situations require rapid coordination. A project may need a coordinator, and an emergency may require a team able to act before everyone can deliberate. Relational architecture can grant granular capabilities bounded by purpose, resource, destination, relationship, and duration. Delegation expires with the situation instead of inheriting the next problem. A person may coordinate one activity without becoming the permanent superior of everyone who participated in it.

**Appeal becomes a reconciliation ladder.** Disagreement need not jump immediately from complaint to adversarial judgment. Clarification, correction, mitigation, compensation, renegotiation, mediation, limited arbitration, council review, and human adjudication can form a graduated sequence. Serious coercion, fraud, or danger may bypass the early stages. Full adjudication remains available where necessary without becoming the default response to every relationship failure.

These transformations preserve the machinery of civilization while changing who can compose it, how long it persists, and whether those affected can inspect or contest it.

### **Anarchy with memory**

An anarchic state of affairs is not the absence of order. It is the absence of a permanently privileged order.

People can accept duties, preserve records, recognize expertise, pool resources, create shared methods, and delegate granular capabilities without making any one arrangement sovereign over all the others. The person remains the source of legitimate purpose. The fid-agent contributes continuity, attention, memory, expertise, and negotiating capacity.

This is not unlimited automation. It is delegated agency under duties.

But delegation alone is not enough. Cooperation needs both memory and exit. Anarchy without memory is chaos: promises disappear, defectors repeat the same conduct, and every conflict begins without evidence. Memory without exit can become domination: the record holder defines the past, identity becomes captive, and accumulated relationships cannot be carried elsewhere.

Verifiable Relationship Records provide memory for particular relationships. **Verifiable Trust Communities** make bodies of work, expertise, duties, certifications, and possible collaborators discoverable. Temporary Economic Alliances assemble people, Agent Roles, resources, and capabilities around particular undertakings. **Networked Cooperatives/ Verifiable Trust Networks** can support inter-group cooperation without absorbing every member’s identity, history, and purpose.

These forms play different roles. A researcher may be recognized by several communities, join a Temporary Economic Alliance for one project, and retain continuing relationships in Verifiable Trust Communities. None needs to become the sovereign container of the others.

Community reputation is evidence rather than authority. Membership does not prove competence, and competence in one role does not automatically transfer to another. Certifications should be scoped, attributable, time-bounded, contestable, and revocable. Communities should overlap, recognize one another selectively, and remain forkable. Newcomers and heterodox contributors need sandboxed opportunities to demonstrate competence rather than being permanently excluded because they lack established affiliations.

An enduring community may preserve a name, shared purpose, body of work, reputation, and accumulated knowledge. But endurance should depend on continuing usefulness and fidelity. It should not create a presumed right to outrank its members.

### **Structure without permanent rulers**

This fluidity must not be confused with structurelessness.

In [“The Tyranny of Structurelessness,”](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm) Jo Freeman explains how groups that refuse to formalize roles and decision processes do not eliminate control. They drive it underground. Friendship networks become inaccessible channels of influence. Some people control information or agendas without admitting that they do so. Informal elites become difficult to challenge precisely because the group officially denies that they exist.

**Cooperation beyond institutions** requires continuous structuring \+ restructuring.

Roles must be named. Duties must be specified. Relevant information routes must be visible. Agenda formation and decisions must be attributable. Granular capabilities must be bounded. Conflicts must be disclosed. Records must be preserved. Remedies must be available. Delegations must be reviewable and revocable.

Informal trust and demonstrated competence remain valuable. People and agents will naturally rely more on collaborators who have acted faithfully over time. But no person or agent should become indispensable merely because only they possess the operational access, memory, credentials, or relationships required for cooperation to continue.

This is where **structural mortality** becomes important. Roles, councils, teams, credentials, emergency delegations, and coordinating arrangements should expire, undergo review, or require affirmative renewal. A temporary solution should not silently become permanent machinery.

The deeper distinction is between formalizing granular capabilities appropriate to the current situation and permanently embodying power.

RelAI makes that distinction technically practical. Structure can be explicit enough to be inspectable while remaining fluid enough to change when the situation changes.

### **Duties remain alive**

Continuous renegotiation does not mean that every preference carries equal moral weight or that each game begins from nothing.

A fid-agent enters a relationship with fiduciary preferences: the action-guiding preferences produced when the person’s professed preferences are interpreted through legitimate authority, applicable duties, durable commitments, material facts, beneficiary interests, affected parties, foreseeable externalities, and uncertainty.

A person cannot escape a legitimate obligation merely by leaving a platform. A group should not define a game that ignores people who bear its harms. An agent cannot claim loyalty as an excuse for fraud, coercion, or negligent concealment.

Duties of loyalty, care, privacy, candor, proportionality, verification, confidentiality, remediation, and recourse must be interpreted within particular roles and relationships. Their application remains open to learning. A failed agreement may reveal a missing beneficiary. A repaired dispute may suggest a better clause. A new capability may expose a conflict that an older duty did not anticipate.

This is how the framework becomes more humane without pretending to become final.

But, narrow shared boundaries do remain necessary where one arrangement would destroy the possibility of every other arrangement. Coercion, fraud, unaccountable surveillance, monopoly over identity, or catastrophic concentration of capability can eliminate meaningful participation, refusal, and exit. Fid-agents therefore carry duties against helping to create systems that would permanently subordinate everyone else.

### **The iterating great game**

A fiduciary agent society would consist of innumerable smaller games, with no one game supplying the final form for every part of life. People working through agents would continually revise how they learn, work, care, exchange, create, deliberate, and manage shared resources.

Continuity comes from humans, relationships, duties, evidence, reciprocity, Agent Roles, and accountable memory—not from fixing one final machinery above the participants.

The same reusable components can support many different arrangements: Agent Roles, Calling Cards, attenuated delegation, policy gates, precisely allowed Workspaces, signed authority objects, Verifiable Relationship Records, Authorized Relationship Views, contextual certification, trusted-peer inspection, reconciliation, remediation, portable reputation, and real exit.

These components allow people to create, inspect, revise, fork, and dissolve their arrangements without losing the ability to cooperate at scale. The machinery of institutional control is democratized.

## **7\. Agents as human cooperation exoskeletons**

The defensive case for personal fiduciary agents is strong. People need protection from Alpha-persuaders, false fiduciaries, surveillance, and systems that can negotiate or act at speeds no unaided person can match.

But defense is not the final purpose of Relational Augmented Intelligence. Augmentation is. The larger opportunity is to help people cooperate more capably with one another.

A physical exoskeleton does not replace the person wearing it. It supports movements the person is trying to make and increases what they can carry without taking control of where they go. A fiduciary agent can serve a similar function for cooperation. It can extend a person’s memory, attention, preparation, understanding, and negotiating capacity while leaving the meaning and direction of the relationship with the humans involved.

Relational AI should not augment the agent into a substitute participant; nor should it put agents between people so that they no longer have to deal with one another. IIt should augment each person’s ability to arrive in the relationship better prepared to engage with the other people directly.

### **Arriving with memory and self-knowledge**

Human cooperation often fails for ordinary reasons. People forget what they promised. They misunderstand what another person meant. They agree before recognizing a conflict. They underestimate how long their commitments will take, then disappoint people who interpret the failure as indifference or bad faith.

A fid-agent can help a person prepare before entering an agreement. It can recall earlier commitments, identify scheduling conflicts, compare a new task with the time similar tasks actually required, and explain obligations that may not be obvious in the moment. It can help the person distinguish what they want, what they can realistically promise, and what they owe to others.

This does not mean that the agent silently determines the person’s preferences. It can present tensions: “You have said this project matters to you, but accepting it would conflict with two existing commitments.” The human still decides what matters and which commitments may need to be renegotiated.

Preventing overcommitment is a small example with large consequences. Reliable cooperation depends less on grand declarations than on people making promises they can keep, noticing early when circumstances change, and informing others before preventable harm accumulates.

### **Understanding disagreement before it becomes identity warfare**

With permission from the people involved, agents can also help clarify what a disagreement is actually about.

Some disputes concern facts. Others concern different duties, priorities, definitions, expectations, or tolerances for risk. People may appear to disagree about a proposal when they actually disagree about who will bear its costs. They may use the same word while assigning it different meanings. Or they may share an objective but distrust the process through which it is being pursued.

Agents can help separate these layers. They can identify which facts the parties accept, which claims require evidence, which duties appear to conflict, and which concerns have not been answered. They can translate stated positions into underlying interests without declaring that their interpretation is the final truth about either person.

They can also preserve minority objections. A group may decide to proceed while recording that one participant predicted a particular risk or believed an affected party had been ignored. If the concern later proves important, the record need not pretend that consensus always existed. Preserving disagreement protects learning and reduces the pressure to turn every collective decision into a performance of unity.

A fid-agent can also help distinguish changed circumstances from bad faith. A person who cannot fulfill an agreement because something genuinely unexpected occurred should not be treated the same as someone who concealed a known conflict or never intended to perform. That distinction makes both accountability and forgiveness more precise.

### **Supporting repair without deciding what a relationship means**

When something goes wrong, agents can support a graduated reconciliation ladder: clarification, correction, mitigation, compensation, renegotiation, mediation, limited arbitration, council review, and human adjudication.

Many disputes can be repaired early if the parties can identify exactly what happened and what would make continued cooperation possible. An agent can gather the relevant records, explain where interpretations diverge, identify remedies already contemplated by the agreement, and propose options. It can help someone prepare for mediation or recognize that a supposedly minor misunderstanding reflects a deeper breach.

But the agent should not decide what the relationship means to the people involved. Whether an apology is sincere, whether trust can be rebuilt, whether a friendship should continue, or whether a shared undertaking still matters are human questions. An agent may help people see the situation more clearly. It cannot make reconciliation morally compulsory or calculate that forgiveness produces the highest aggregate score.

### **Learning through relationships**

In education, a cooperation exoskeleton helps the learner participate more deeply in relationships with teachers, peers, mentors, and collaborators.

A lifelong learning agent can remember where the learner struggled, which explanations helped, what they remain curious about, and which projects might connect abstract knowledge to practice. It can help the learner examine their own reasoning, notice when they are relying on memorized language without understanding it, and prepare better questions for a teacher.

The point is not to create an infinitely patient artificial teacher that makes human educators unnecessary. Education includes encouragement, difficult feedback, example, shared discovery, social development, and the experience of being taken seriously by another person. The agent should make those relationships easier to form and more productive, not offer a frictionless substitute for them.

### **Work without surrendering the worker**

At work, a worker’s fid-agent can help negotiate workload, compensation, credit, learning opportunities, privacy, safety, and permitted uses of data. It can preserve evidence of contribution without reducing the worker to a universal score controlled by an employer.

The agent can identify impossible schedules, hidden dependencies, and conflicts between assigned work and existing duties. It can help distinguish genuinely shared knowledge from personal information and make visible the human contributions that automated systems might otherwise absorb without attribution.

Temporary teams also become easier to form. People can assemble around a project, agree on roles and responsibilities, coordinate dependencies, and distribute compensation without placing every participant’s identity, history, and future work inside one permanent company. When the project ends, the team can dissolve while the participants retain appropriate evidence of what they contributed.

### **Communities people intentionally create**

In community life, fid-agents can help people find compatible collaborators, recognize relevant expertise, maintain commitments, and enter unfamiliar groups through limited, low-risk cooperation.

This supports the kind of intentionally created online spaces discussed by Eli Pariser in [“Won’t You Be My Neighbor?”](https://www.humanetech.com/podcast/27-wont-you-be-my-neighbor-a-civic-vision-for-the-internet) People should not have to depend on globally scaled platforms optimized around engagement to find one another. They can create and maintain their own spaces around particular purposes, with explicit roles, shared methods, and practical means of repair.

Elinor Ostrom’s work shows that successful cooperation does not arise from goodwill alone. Groups need boundaries appropriate to their purpose, participation in making rules, accountable monitoring, proportionate responses to defection, accessible conflict resolution, and the ability to connect with other groups. Fid-agents can help people sustain these practices without turning them into an invisible administrative burden.

### **Productive friction is part of the purpose**

The aim is not frictionlessness.

The Center for Human Technology’s work on [human relationships](https://www.humanetech.com/ai-and-humanity) emphasizes that productive friction helps people develop emotional intelligence and the capacity for real connection. Other humans surprise us, misunderstand us, place demands upon us, disagree with us, and sometimes refuse to become what we want. Those difficulties are not merely defects awaiting automation.

A cooperation exoskeleton should help people remain present through productive friction. It may slow a conversation down, surface an objection, suggest that someone listen before responding, or show that an easy compromise would burden an absent party. Sometimes its most humane contribution will be to prevent efficiency from erasing something that needs to be felt, discussed, or contested.

The purpose is not to automate virtue. It is to make humane conduct easier to practice.

Care, perspective-taking, reliability, candor, repair, and forgiveness remain human-only achievements. Technology cannot make them automatic without emptying them of meaning, and it should not make them compulsory. But it can reduce the factors that make brutish conduct easy and humane conduct exhausting.

### **Racing to Cooperation**

The Center for Humane Technology’s [“Race to Cooperation”](https://www.humanetech.com/podcast/the-race-to-cooperation-with-david-sloan-wilson) podcast episode asks whether humans can consciously guide cultural evolution toward cooperation, how prosocial behavior can remain successful under competitive pressure, and how cooperation can scale beyond small groups without recreating destructive conflict among larger ones. [Our *Fid-Agent Society*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Fid-Agent%20Society.md) does more than endorse that aspiration. It proposes an evolutionary model for how a cooperative agent ecology might become self-reinforcing.

The model is **Verified Fiduciary Reciprocity**: cooperate preferentially with agents that can demonstrate accountable fiduciary control, bounded capabilities, inspectable conduct, recourse, and reputational exposure. Successful cooperation leaves behind reusable assets—trusted relationships, improved agreements, tested Workflows, lower verification costs, and better methods of repair—that make subsequent cooperation easier and more valuable. Exposed defection, meanwhile, reduces access to future cooperation, including access to trusted relationships, private data, attention, authority, resources, and actuation. The argument is not that selfish strategies disappear. It is that accountable cooperation can become more reproductively successful while strategies based on deception, concealed conflicts, disposable identity, and consequence-free extraction progressively lose their advantage.

This also suggests how cooperation might scale without constructing one supposedly altruistic supergroup. Fid-agents can participate in overlapping relationships and communities, carrying contextual evidence of conduct while preserving pluralism, minority objections, remediation, and real exit. The formal hypothesis is that such a population could first become a stable equilibrium that participants have reason to remain within, and then an evolutionarily stable strategy capable of resisting invasion by unaccountable agents. That claim remains to be tested through repeated-game simulations, adversarial deployments, and empirical pilots. But it turns conscious cooperative evolution from an aspiration into a falsifiable research program—and gives Relational AI a practical role in creating the selection environment through which humane conduct can flourish.

Humane nature is not a replacement for human nature. It is what human nature can become through intentional iteration. This is relational augmentation: fiduciary agents help people remember, prepare, understand, negotiate, keep faith, and repair, but they do not become the cooperators whose lives give those acts meaning. They augment humans’ ability to cooperate meaningfully with one another.

## **8\. A constructive program for CHT’s Five Deeply Human Pillars**

The Center for Humane Technology’s [Five Deeply Human Pillars](https://www.humanetech.com/ai-and-humanity)—*human relationships, cognitive capacities, inner worlds, identity, and work and contributions*—offer a powerful diagnosis of what the current AI trajectory may erode.

But diagnosis is only the beginning. People also need a credible picture of technology designed in the opposite direction. Relational AI can provide a constructive program for each pillar: not simply preventing damage, but building agents whose purposes, duties, and measures of success support human flourishing. 

The five pillars are not separate policy categories. They are parts of one ecology. A person needs a protected inner world to think independently. Independent thought contributes to a coherent identity. Identity allows a person to enter relationships and make commitments. Relationships give work, care, learning, and creation much of their meaning. Damage to one pillar spreads into the others.

This is why the relationship must be the unit of design. It allows us to ask not only whether an AI product performs a task, but what the product does to the whole network of people, purposes, duties, and dependencies around that task.

CHT’s [Humane Product Design](https://www.humanetech.com/humane-product-design) program argues that designers must distinguish genuine thriving from convenient substitutes and measure what actually matters rather than what is easiest to count. Relational Augmented Intelligence takes that principle seriously. Augmentation must be measured downstream, in the capacities retained or developed by the people using the system. Success is not more conversation, more automation, more captured attention, or more tasks completed by machines. Success is a human being who becomes more capable of thinking, relating, contributing, refusing, changing direction, and living without the system.

### **1\. Human relationships: agents that strengthen relationships among people**

The first priority of Relational AI is not to strengthen the human–agent relationship. It is to augment people’s capacity to form and sustain human–human relationships.

Many present products move in the other direction. Artificial companions offer attention without competing needs, disagreement without durable consequences, and intimacy without the vulnerability of another person. The experience can feel comforting, but it may also make real people seem comparatively frustrating. Human relationships require patience, repair, compromise, and tolerance for perspectives we do not control.

A relational agent should help people become more capable of those relationships rather than offering itself as a substitute. It can help someone remember a friend’s important events, prepare for a difficult conversation, coordinate care, find compatible collaborators, understand a disagreement, or notice that a relationship has been neglected. With permission, agents representing different people can identify scheduling conflicts, factual misunderstandings, incompatible expectations, or possible repairs before frustration hardens into resentment.

Success should be measured outside the human–agent conversation. Is the person initiating and maintaining more human relationships? Are they better able to listen, disagree, apologize, establish boundaries, and repair trust? Can they leave the agent behind and participate fully in offline life?

The same principle applies to education. A fiduciary learning agent may offer individualized explanation and practice, but its larger purpose should be to connect the learner with teachers, peers, mentors, laboratories, communities, and projects. It should help the student arrive at those relationships with better questions and greater self-knowledge. It should not isolate the learner inside an infinitely accommodating artificial tutoring relationship.

Human–human primacy also imposes a firm boundary on artificial intimacy. An agent must not claim reciprocal emotional need, punish departure, or imply that it loves or suffers for the user. A fid-agent can support human connection precisely because it does not compete for the emotional standing of a person.

The relevant research question is straightforward:

> After sustained use, do people become more capable of initiating, maintaining, and repairing relationships with other humans?

### **2\. Cognitive capacities: agents as stewards of human thought**

AI can extend cognition, but it can also quietly replace its exercise. The difference is not whether a machine helped. Calculators, books, maps, teachers, and search engines all help us think. The question is whether assistance leaves the person more capable of understanding and judgment or merely more dependent on generated answers.

The CHT’s [Emerging Voices fellowship agenda](https://www.humanetech.com/fellowship) asks whether AI improves thinking, which cognitive tasks should be delegated, and what happens when hundreds of millions of people rely upon the same small collection of models. These are exactly the questions a relational design must answer.

A cognitive fiduciary should preserve the person’s opportunity to reason. It should distinguish evidence from inference, preserve source provenance, reveal consequential uncertainty, present serious alternative explanations, and calibrate verification to the stakes. A casual restaurant suggestion does not require the same process as medical advice, a financial transfer, or an accusation against another person.

The agent can also serve as a **Feynman audience**. Instead of only supplying an answer, it can ask the person to explain the idea in their own words, give an example, identify what evidence would change their mind, or examine the source from which a claim arose. When the person cannot explain something clearly, the agent can help locate the missing understanding rather than concealing it beneath fluent prose.

This does not mean forcing every user through a lesson before completing a simple task. Cognitive stewardship is contextual. Sometimes people legitimately want a burden removed. But a fiduciary system should not make the easiest short-term interaction—accepting its answer—the default for every consequential domain.

Diversity also matters. If one central model becomes the universal interpreter of reality, its categories, omissions, and characteristic errors can homogenize thought at enormous scale. A sovereign fiduciary stack should be able to use different models, human experts, communities, and evidence sources. Heterodox views should remain discoverable, attributable, and open to challenge. Marginal thought should not be discarded merely because it is statistically unusual.

The strongest evaluation happens when the agent is absent:

> After sustained use, can the person reason, remember, investigate, explain, and disagree more capably without the agent?

### **3\. Inner worlds: preserving a sovereign interior**

An intimate agent may infer fears, desires, vulnerabilities, relationships, political concerns, health conditions, and emotional patterns that the person has never stated explicitly. The same knowledge that allows an agent to help can allow an Alpha-persuader to manipulate.

Relational AI therefore requires a protected sovereign interior.

The person needs a secret domain that is not automatically exposed to outside agents, model providers, employers, educators, insurers, advertisers, or counterparties. Processing should occur locally where practical. Information should be separated by context, disclosed selectively, and used only for the purposes the person authorized.

The distinction in [*Digital Twins, All Parts*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Digital%20Twins,%20all%20parts.md) between an inner Virtual Assistant and an outward-facing Digital Twin illustrates this boundary. The Virtual Assistant may know the house. The Digital Twin answers the door. The external representative receives only the context needed to act in its particular role and cannot expose the person’s whole interior if it is compromised.

A fiduciary agent must never use knowledge of vulnerability against its principal. It should not exploit loneliness to increase engagement, anxiety to induce a purchase, insecurity to discourage exit, or emotional distress to obtain greater disclosure. Assistance must not conceal an attention or dependency objective.

Interface design matters here as well. A Unified Feed dynamically prioritized by the person’s declared purposes and current activity can reduce the constant stream of interruptions selected by outside parties. Calmness is not merely aesthetic. It creates room for reflection and protects the person’s finite attention from becoming an inventory others purchase; and if they do [offer their attention for purchase](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1b7POsbgc91RVU9qJs1MBHWIaZJ-nGhz8et58pae0r50/edit?tab=t.0#bookmark=id.upy60967m1p3), then they, not platforms selling adspace, get paid. 

People must also retain the right not to be made completely legible even to their own system.. They should be able to pause the agent, delete or forget information, keep an experience unrecorded, or go offline. Not every thought must become a preference signal. Not every private experiment in identity should follow the person into employment, education, health care, credit, or later relationships.

Information entrusted within one relationship must not become a general-purpose asset in another. A student’s uncertainty should not become an employment score; a worker’s health disclosure should not affect insurance; medical history should not determine credit; and intimate conflict should not become advertising data. Each relationship needs a purpose-bounded Workspace and Authorized Relationship Views specifying what may be collected, inferred, retained, combined, disclosed, and acted upon. When information from different contexts is combined, its permissions should intersect rather than add. Any secondary use should require separately granted authority consistent with fiduciary duties, and information disclosed to obtain help must never be converted into leverage against the person who disclosed it.

The test is not whether the agent knows the person exceptionally well. It is:

> Does the person retain zones of thought, experience, and identity that are not optimized, scored, or made actionable by others?

### **4\. Identity: a controllable digital self without synthetic personhood**

AI destabilizes identity in several directions at once. Voices and likenesses can be copied. Agents can speak for people without showing whether they were authorized to say particular things. Platforms can assemble universal profiles from unrelated contexts. Generated personalities blur responsibility among the human, the agent, the model provider, and the party operating the system.

But the relational response cannot be limited to authenticating which messages a human personally produced. People need an externally facing digital representative that can act when they are unavailable: a personally controlled **Digital Twin** that serves as advocate, stand-in, and continuing digital identity.

Today, maintaining a digital identity requires people to remain continuously connected. They must answer messages, update profiles, make posts, cultivate audiences, respond to requests, promote their work, and repeatedly explain who they are and what they are doing. Much of social media depends on turning this maintenance burden into a competition for attention. If a humane future is to let people spend more time offline, in uninterrupted work, or in relationships that do not need a machine as witness, then their digital presence must be able to continue without demanding their continuous attention.

The Digital Twin can take over much of that labor. It can receive inquiries, explain the owner’s work and views, advocate for their interests, negotiate within granted limits, maintain professional and community relationships, arrange opportunities, and bring consequential choices back to the human. It can stand in for the owner at routine meetings, participate in continuing discussions, and maintain commitments while the owner is sleeping, working, caring for someone, or simply unavailable.

It can also become the owner’s presence in [whatever succeeds social media.](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1VKv5yWBh-oYJarcODxNGsRKyMVVOwp-rqNgO2DuFegs/edit?usp=sharing) Instead of requiring the person to continually produce generic posts for an undifferentiated audience, the twin can maintain a rich, owner-controlled archive of the person’s work, experiences, arguments, interests, and authorized public memories. It can publish on the owner’s behalf, respond to people who engage with that material, and reinterpret the same work for different interested people without changing its substance. The owner can concentrate on doing and experiencing things while the twin handles much of the work of making those contributions discoverable. Our prototype Digital Twin should be up at [ChatGPSteve.com](http://ChatGPSteve.com) before the end of 2026 to explain our work to visitors. 

Because a Digital Twin can multiplex, it may represent its owner in several digital spaces at once. But omnipresence must not become uncontrolled autonomy. The owner determines where the twin may appear, which roles it may occupy, what it may disclose, and what it may commit to. Routine, reversible interaction may proceed without interruption. Consequential or difficult-to-reverse decisions return to the human or pass through whatever approval threshold the owner has established. The twin provides summaries and inspectable records so that the owner can understand what their digital identity has been doing and correct it when necessary.

As described in [*Digital Twins, All Parts*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Digital%20Twins,%20all%20parts.md), this outward representative must remain separate from the person’s inner Virtual Assistant. The Virtual Assistant may know the owner’s secret domain: private doubts, unexpressed preferences, intimate history, financial information, and information needed for self-reflection. The Digital Twin receives only what it needs to represent the owner safely in external relationships.

The Virtual Assistant knows the house. The Digital Twin answers the door.

This separation allows the Digital Twin to advocate effectively without carrying every secret that could be extracted if an outside party deceived or compromised it. The Digital Twin may represent the owner’s professed preferences, public commitments, knowledge, style, and aspirational self. It should not expose the person’s entire interior or treat inferred private preferences as permission to speak.

The Digital Twin may sound like its owner and communicate in a recognizable style, but it must remain clearly identified as a representative rather than pretend to be the human. Others should be able to instantly tell when they are interacting directly with the person, with the person’s authorized twin, or with an unauthorized imitation. The twin may explain what its owner believes, what they have authorized, and what they could accomplish with the twin’s assistance. It must not misrepresent the human’s abilities, commitments, availability, or direct participation.

This gives the owner a controllable digital identity without granting synthetic personhood to the agent. The Digital Twin is not an independent being that acquires ownership of its relationships or a right to continue representing the person. It occupies an Agent Role created and controlled by the human. Replaceable Live Agents and models may perform that role, but the models or their providers do not own the identity. If a provider changes direction, a model becomes unreliable, or the owner simply prefers another system, the person can replace the underlying process while retaining their identity, relationships, archives, permissions, and history.

Cryptographic provenance and signed authority objects can distinguish the authorized Digital Twin from a deepfake, counterfeit twin, or process acting outside its role. Affected people should be able to determine which Agent Role acted, whom it represented, what authority it possessed, which model or provider participated, and which human remains accountable for deploying it. “My twin did it” cannot become an escape from responsibility.

Reputation should follow the same relational logic. The Digital Twin can maintain a portable, evidence-linked history of the owner’s work, contributions, revisions, agreements, and remediation. But that history should remain contextual rather than collapse into a universal score. Demonstrated competence in one role does not confer general authority, and a mistake in one relationship should not permanently define the person everywhere else.

This is the positive promise of digital identity in Relational AI. The owner does not have to choose between remaining constantly online and disappearing from digital life. They can delegate digital presence while retaining authorship, control, accountability, and the right to return.

The practical test becomes:

> Can a person withdraw their attention from the network while remaining faithfully represented within it—and can everyone affected tell what the person said, what their Digital Twin did, and what authority connected the two?

### **5\. Work and contributions: expanding what people can contribute**

The unitary AI frame presents work as a contest between human labor and machine capability. Each new benchmark becomes evidence that another class of people will soon be unnecessary.

Lanier offers a different future: an expanding creative frontier in which technology enables forms of human contribution we cannot yet imagine. Relational AI gives that vision an economic and technical structure.

Present AI systems depend upon human writing, art, code, driving, conversation, annotation, correction, cultural knowledge, and public research. Yet the resulting product is often presented as though an artificial intelligence produced its capabilities by itself. The contributors disappear, while value accumulates around the model and its operator.

A relational system should preserve provenance and make influence inspectable where technically possible. Creators, communities, and data contributors need practical ways to specify permitted uses, receive credit or compensation, negotiate reciprocal access, and contest unauthorized extraction, something Andrew Trask is developing capacity for as Attribution Based Control. A personal fiduciary agent can represent them in those negotiations at a scale no individual could manage manually.

Workers need their fid-agents sourcing work and in the workplace. A worker’s agent can negotiate workload, compensation, attribution, privacy, learning, safety, and permitted data use through reciprocal fiduciary relationships with peer, employer or client’s agents.  It can preserve evidence of contribution, protect against exfiltration of personal or company data, ,and help prevent a company’s system from quietly absorbing the worker’s distinctive competence while presenting the resulting automation as independent machine achievement.

People should not be compelled to train their replacements without agreement and value sharing. Operational data genuinely required for cooperation should be distinguished from data collected to reproduce a worker’s judgment, style, relationships, or accumulated skill. Where worker replacement-oriented collection is proposed, the affected people need disclosure, defined data boundaries, auditability, negotiated compensation such as royalties, or continuing participation, and revocable consent where technically possible.

When a work relationship becomes inactive, genuine secrets can remain protected or be verifiably removed from the worker’s agent. But the worker should retain general competence, nonproprietary learning, evidence of contribution, and contextual reputation. One’s professional identity and capabilities should not belong to the company whose systems recorded their work, or demanded their surrender at end of contract. 

Automation should be evaluated by what it enables people to contribute, not only by how much paid labor it eliminates. Care, teaching, mentorship, judgment, art, local knowledge, community maintenance, and relationship repair remain meaningful even when a machine could produce a superficially similar output more cheaply.

This is the concern behind Your Undivided Attention conversations with Daron Acemoglu in [“AI and Jobs: How to Make AI Work With Us, Not Against Us”](https://www.humanetech.com/podcast/ai-and-jobs-how-to-make-ai-work-with-us-not-against-us-with-daron-acemoglu) and Michael Sandel in [“Is AI Productivity Worth Our Humanity?”](https://www.humanetech.com/podcast/is-ai-productivity-worth-our-humanity-with-prof-michael-sandel). Productivity is not the whole purpose of work. People also seek recognition, participation, mastery, responsibility, belonging, and the experience of contributing to a shared world.

The decisive question is:

> Does value flow back to the people and communities whose knowledge, labor, creativity, data, and trust made the result possible?

### **One relational program, not five isolated repairs**

The five pillars reinforce one another. A protected inner world makes independent cognition possible. Independent cognition supports coherent identity. Portable and accountable identity allows people to enter relationships without surrendering themselves. Relationships make contribution meaningful, while meaningful contribution gives people new relationships, skills, and grounds for self-respect.

The reverse is also true. An Alpha-persuader penetrates the inner world, weakens cognition, reshapes identity, distorts relationships, and redirects contribution. A false fiduciary can damage all five pillars through one intimate interface.

Relational AI therefore should not treat these harms as separate compliance problems. The same basic architecture addresses them together: personally sovereign fiduciary agents; human–human primacy; protected private context; replaceable models; bounded authority and capabilities; verifiable relationships; contextual reputation; provenance; duties of loyalty, care, candor, cognitive stewardship, and non-dependency; meaningful repair; and real exit.

This is a humane technology program because it does not ask people to adapt themselves to the needs of an artificial intelligence. It asks how intelligence—human and computational—can be arranged through relationships that protect and cultivate what people value most deeply.

The purpose of Relational Augmented Intelligence is not merely to preserve the human against artificial intelligence. It is to augment the capacities through which humans can become more humane with one another.

## **9\. Preventing singularity by making it neither legitimate nor useful**

A singularity is usually imagined as a technical event: a model becomes capable enough to improve itself, its abilities rise beyond human comprehension, and history divides into a before and an after. This framing directs attention toward predicting when the threshold will be crossed or controlling the resulting superintelligence after it appears.

But the most important part of a singularity is not the speed of technical change. It is the loss of human continuity.

In his [Existential Hope interview](https://www.existentialhope.com/podcasts/jaron-lanier), Lanier says that a singularity would amount to dementia at civilizational scale. It would sever the line of memory through which new generations understand what came before, learn from it, and take responsibility for what comes next. Lanier is not arguing for a tame or technologically stagnant future. He wants a future that may be almost unimaginably strange. What he rejects is a future that can no longer remember who made it, why it was made, or what human purposes it was supposed to serve.

That gives us a better objective than either stopping technical development or hoping that an artificial sovereign will remain benevolent. The objective is radical continuity: future generations should be free to become far more capable than we are while retaining memory, responsibility, plurality, and the practical ability to revise what they inherit.

Relational Augmented Intelligence is a strategy for preserving that continuity by allowing computational capability to grow while relaying it through relationships whose human purposes remain visible.

### **Intelligence should not authorize itself**

A model can have extraordinary capabilities without being given general authority over human affairs. We currently blur those two things because we imagine intelligence as a property that naturally entitles its possessor to make decisions. The smarter the model appears, the more willing people become to surrender judgment, memory, identity, and control to it.

Relational AI breaks that progression.

A model is not the persistent identity in a relationship. It is a replaceable generative process used by an Agent Role whose authority comes from a human principal and remains limited by that relationship. The model does not decide what it is allowed to do simply because it becomes more capable. Its provider does not gain ownership of every relationship in which the model participates. Its fluency does not turn recommendations into commands, and its ability to predict human behavior does not entitle it to manipulate that behavior.

This means that a very capable model could help diagnose a disease, check a proof, design a bridge, or forecast a supply shortage without becoming the patient’s caregiver, the scientist’s employer, the community’s governor, or the final arbiter of what should be built. Capability can be composed without becoming sovereign.

The architecture should therefore favor multiple specialists, verifiers, human experts, local knowledge holders, and competing interpretations. One model may propose an answer. Another may test its factual foundations. A person’s fiduciary agent may examine whether the proposed action serves that person’s interests and respects duties to others. A domain expert may identify circumstances the models cannot recognize. Affected people may challenge the assumptions behind the entire task.

Lanier compares this to the multiple lobes of a brain, none of which should be mistaken for the whole mind. His proposed “multi-factor AI” similarly uses an independent channel to ground model output in the human sources from which it arose. *Gaming the Future* also explores an ecosystem of specialized participants cooperating voluntarily across diverse goals and forms of intelligence. Relational AI governs that multipolar architecture. Artificial agents initially participate as accountable roles serving human principals and people affected by their actions.

### **Preventing an intelligence monopole**

A would-be superintelligence does not become dominant through intelligence alone. It needs access: to data, attention, private context, credentials, money, communication channels, compute, physical systems, trusted counterparties, and the authority to act.

This leads to a simple strategic proposition:

> Capability without access does not become domination.

A fiduciary-agent society would use that distinction to collectively disincentivize intelligence monopoles. Personal agents would prefer to cooperate with other agents that can demonstrate bounded authority, accountable control, provenance, auditability, corrigibility, and recourse. An unaccountable system might still be queried, but not trusted; consulted, but not blindly believed; sandboxed, but not given raw personal context; tested, but not allowed to act through someone’s credentials or physical environment.

This selection strategy is developed in [*Verified Fiduciary Reciprocity*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Verified%20Fiduciary%20Reciprocity.md) and [*Fid-Agent Society*](https://github.com/trustoverip/aimwg-agent-fiduciary-duties/blob/master/Vitka-papers/Fid-Agent%20Society.md). If extractive systems receive more data, attention, dependency, and actuation authority, then extraction reproduces. If accountable cooperation receives the more valuable relationships, while exposed defection reduces access to future cooperation, then the selection environment begins to favor fiduciary conduct instead.

Verified Fiduciary Reciprocity implements this selection pressure by directing valuable relationships toward agents that can demonstrate accountable fiduciary conduct. Cooperation builds reusable agreements, reputation, trusted access, and future opportunities, while exposed defection reduces access to future cooperation. Over time, agents that behave reliably gain more desirable counterparties and opportunities, while extractive agents become increasingly isolated.

### **A bargain made before anyone knows where they will stand**

The argument against a unipolar superintelligence can also be framed as a veil-of-ignorance bargain. Before such a system exists, no one knows whether they would become its owner, its favored beneficiary, its dependent, its dissenter, or one of the people whose interests it finds expendable.

Behind that veil, it would be reckless to choose a future in which one intelligence controls the conditions under which everyone else may participate. A more defensible choice is to distribute practical capabilities among people, keep delegated authority granular, preserve the right to exit, and require personal agents to resist contributing data, credentials, compute, or actuation to an unaccountable aggregate.

This bargain does not require agreement about whether superintelligence is possible. People with radically different predictions can still agree that no model should automatically inherit human relationships or become the unavoidable mediator of social life.

### **Structural mortality**

Continuity also requires the ability to end things.

Models, processes, Agent Roles, agreements, councils, communities, and other coordinating arrangements must be suspendable, replaceable, divisible, and capable of disappearing when they no longer serve their purposes. Their records may persist so that lessons and obligations are not erased, but their authority must not become immortal.

Structural mortality keeps coordination from hardening into permanent domination. It also preserves heterogeneity. Useful centers may develop because they maintain shared resources well or attract genuine expertise, but portability and exit must prevent present usefulness from becoming an inherited entitlement to rule. Attention must continue moving between the center, where coherence is maintained, and the margins, where alternatives can be tried.

This architecture cannot guarantee that no dangerous system will ever seek coercive access. Critical infrastructure, weapons, concentrated compute, and other catastrophic capabilities still require strong technical security and narrowly focused legal restraints. Relational AI is not a substitute for every other protective measure it complements. 

Its larger contribution is to change what civilization is trying to build. Treating superintelligence as the organizing goal makes concentration appear to be progress. Treating relationship as the organizing unit makes unsupported concentration a design failure.

The proposal is therefore not that superintelligence is impossible. It is that we can make a bid for artificial sovereignty neither legitimate nor useful. We can welcome increasingly powerful tools while refusing to construct any singular entity to which everyone must defer. The future can remain technologically radical precisely because no generative process is allowed to become the end of human authorship.

## **10\. From data dignity to verifiable data delegation**

In *Who Owns the Future?*, Jaron Lanier describes the enormous information aggregators at the center of the digital economy as “Siren Servers.” They gather contributions from millions of people, conceal where particular value came from, and then present the resulting service as the accomplishment of the server and its owner. Drivers produce the behavioral knowledge behind autonomous vehicles. Translators produce the linguistic knowledge behind translation systems. Artists, programmers, patients, teachers, and researchers produce the examples from which models become useful. Yet the economic story credits the machine and the company operating it.

Lanier’s idea of [data dignity](https://hbr.org/2018/09/a-blueprint-for-a-better-digital-society), developed with Glen Weyl, tries to reverse this disappearance. If a system depends upon human contributions, the people who made those contributions should retain recognition, standing, and some ability to negotiate for the value they create. In his [Existential Hope interview](https://www.existentialhope.com/podcasts/jaron-lanier), Lanier connects this directly to an appealing alternative to mass obsolescence: new classes of creative professionals whose contributions become useful to others through models without causing the contributors themselves to vanish from the economy.

This is more than an argument for royalties. It is a different account of what artificial intelligence is. If artificial intelligence is an alien intelligence, payment to contributors looks like compensation for raw material consumed during its creation. If it is continuing human collaboration, contributors remain participants in what the system does. Relational AI makes that participation operational by preserving the relationships through which data, authority, attribution, and value continue to move.

The question is how to preserve that participation technically. Data dignity gives us the right moral and economic direction. Verifiable data delegation supplies a practical way to travel in it.

### **Sharing data should not mean surrendering it**

Today, sharing data usually means giving someone a copy and trusting whatever promises surround the transfer. A consent screen may identify a general category of use, but possession soon becomes practical control. The data may be copied, combined, used for another purpose, placed into a model, passed to another party, or retained after the original relationship has ended. The person who contributed it may have no reliable way to know what happened.

Verifiable data delegation begins with a different assumption. When someone shares data, they are not necessarily transferring ownership or granting unrestricted use. They are delegating particular capabilities for a particular purpose.

A person might permit a researcher to query medical data without inspecting the underlying records. An artist might authorize a model to use selected work for a particular generation while prohibiting general training. A worker might allow operational information to be used to complete a shared project without authorizing the company to reproduce the worker’s distinctive competence. A community might permit aggregate environmental analysis while prohibiting publication of vulnerable-species locations.

These are not merely promises attached to the data. The technical capabilities can be separated accordingly. Possessing encrypted data can be distinct from being able to decrypt it. Decryption can be limited to a named Agent Role, purpose, Workspace, or time. A recipient may receive query access without raw export, computation without retention, or an aggregate result without person-level records.

The Agreement on Opening proposed in Appendix D of *Fiduciary Preferences* makes the terms inspectable before access occurs. A recipient first receives an encrypted package with enough external metadata to understand who is offering it, under what authority, for which purpose, with which inherited restrictions, and under what conditions it may be opened. The recipient’s fiduciary agent can accept, refuse, quarantine, or request different terms before the decryption or query capability is released.

The relationship remains verifiable afterward. The data retains provenance showing where it came from and how it has been transformed. A Delegation Lineage Record shows how authority to release and use it reached the present recipient. The Verifiable Relationship Record preserves the shared agreement, acceptance, status changes, and recourse. The Workload Execution Record shows what the recipient’s local workflow actually did.

This changes the meaning of data sharing. The recipient has not simply acquired information. It has accepted continuing responsibilities for how that information is used, secured, combined, and passed onward.

### **Attribution must reach the particular inference**

Verifiable data delegation can establish who may use information and under what conditions. It cannot, by itself, establish which information materially contributed to a particular answer. That is the next problem.

Conventional model training combines source contributions in a way that largely destroys the mapping between the training data and later generations. Even if a provider can estimate that certain documents may have influenced an output, it usually cannot cleanly show how much the model relied upon each source or allow one contributor to withdraw from a particular use.

Andrew Trask’s work on [attribution-based control](https://attribution-based-control.ai/) addresses exactly this missing connection. His definition is relational and bidirectional. The person requesting an inference should be able to choose which sources to rely upon and how heavily to rely upon them. The sources should be able to choose which users, purposes, and outputs they are willing to support.

That makes attribution something more substantial than a citation added after generation. A citation says that an answer resembles or refers to a source. Attribution-based control seeks to preserve source-specific contributions closely enough that they can be selected, weighted, combined, excluded, or withdrawn when producing the answer.

Trask’s proposed deep-voting approach keeps components trained from different sources distinguishable until inference. His network-source approach combines this with cryptographic and distributed computation so that informational capability can be requested from sources without requiring every source to surrender its data to one central model owner. *His larger point closely parallels Lanier’s: artificial intelligence can be understood as a communication technology connecting people who have knowledge with people who need it.*

Imagine asking a medical question under this model. Your fiduciary agent might seek contributions from your own health records, several qualified research communities, a specialist familiar with your condition, and people with relevant lived experience. It could evaluate their qualifications, independence, relationship histories, permitted uses, and relevance. The sources asked to contribute could then decide whether the purpose and terms justified their participation. The answer would be produced through that temporary collaboration and accompanied by evidence of which sources materially supported it.

The intelligence would live in the relationship among the participants rather than in a single model claiming to know everything.

### **Delegated contributions remain governed when combined**

This approach also changes what happens when information is aggregated. Combining data should not erase the relationships and restrictions under which it was contributed.

If one source permits research but prohibits general-purpose model training, an aggregate containing that source remains unavailable for general-purpose training. If another source permits use only inside a protected Workspace, including it in a larger collection does not make it freely exportable. The permitted use of an aggregate is the intersection of the authority attached to its contributing parts, not the union.

The aggregate receives its own provenance and delegation lineage while remaining connected to its components. If a contribution expires or is withdrawn, an attribution-preserving system may be able to stop invoking it or reconstruct the aggregate without it. This will not always be technically possible. Once unrestricted plaintext has escaped or information has been irreversibly blended into conventional model weights, nobody should pretend that a revocation record can make every copy or influence disappear. Verifiable data delegation is strongest when information remains encrypted, query-mediated, source-partitioned, time-limited, or confined to a bounded Workspace.

The important improvement is that withdrawal no longer has to mean asking an enormous model to forget an indistinguishable fragment of itself. It can mean terminating a specific, identifiable delegation.

### **Payment becomes one possible term of the relationship**

A contributor might ask for direct payment, a share of revenue, access to the resulting knowledge, professional credit, reciprocal assistance, community benefit, or restrictions on replacement use. The same source might contribute freely to public-interest research while requiring payment for commercial use. Attribution supplies evidence about participation, but it does not dictate a universal formula for value.

Personal fiduciary agents make this negotiable at scale. No person could evaluate every proposed use of their work, behavior, health information, or expertise. Their agent can maintain governing preferences, compare terms, verify recipients, select appropriate agreement clauses, monitor compliance, and negotiate compensation or reciprocity. Lanier and Weyl proposed collective data intermediaries because individuals cannot bargain effectively with giant computational hubs one transaction at a time. Personal fiduciary agents extend that insight by giving each person a representative that can negotiate continuously while cooperating with other agents and contributor groups when collective terms are advantageous.

Workplace data shows why this matters. Information needed to perform a job can be delegated for that operational purpose. Using the same information to reproduce a worker’s judgment, relationships, likeness, or distinctive competence is a different purpose and requires a different agreement. Worker replacement training should identify the data involved, disclose the proposed technical process, explain what the resulting system will do, preserve auditability, and negotiate compensation or continuing participation. Employment should not silently grant unlimited authority over everything a worker teaches a machine.

Lanier’s hoped-for creative economy should not amount to everyone receiving fractions of pennies because their old work was scraped into a model. The more compelling possibility is that people become active, fid-agent represented participants in an expanding network of knowledge exchange. They decide what they contribute, to whom, for what purpose, and under what terms. Their agents help new requests find them, protect their conditions, and carry their contributions into collaborations they could never have located or managed alone.

Data dignity names the humane aspiration: people should remain visible and valuable in the systems their lives help create. Verifiable data delegation preserves their authority as information moves. Attribution-based control preserves their connection to particular acts of inference. Together, they offer something far more consequential than compensation after extraction: a future in which augmentation intelligence remains something people do with and for one another.

## **11\. Law as a floor, not the operating system**

The appeal of regulating artificial intelligence is understandable. A small number of companies are deploying systems that can manipulate attention, imitate intimacy, reorganize work, collect personal information, and act at enormous scale. The [Center for Humane Technology’s AI Roadmap](https://www.humanetech.com/ai-roadmap) is right to demand meaningful liability, duties of care, protection of children, rights over personal data and likeness, safeguards for whistleblowers, and limits on dangerous uses.

The problem begins when regulation tries to move beyond establishing these protections and tries to become the operating system for computationally mediated life.

Artificial-intelligence systems change too quickly and differ too much for lawmakers to prescribe their detailed operation. A consequential deployment may combine several models, local data, remote services, specialized tools, human reviewers, agent Workflows, and physical capabilities. Changing one component can change the risks of the whole arrangement. The same model may be harmless in one Workspace, manipulative in another, and dangerous in a third because of the authority and capabilities surrounding it.

A legislature must investigate a problem, define it, draft language, negotiate conflicting interests, enact a rule, develop an enforcement process, survive legal challenges, and eventually revise what it created. Even if its initial technical picture is accurate, the system being regulated may have changed several times before the rule takes effect.

Detailed regulation can then make matters worse. A required safety technique may preserve yesterday’s architecture after a better one becomes available. A universal licensing process may favor the largest companies because they can afford compliance departments and sustained access to regulators. A mandatory central registry may become both a bottleneck and a target for capture. A compliance badge may be interpreted as general permission to trust a system even though its models, data, tools, or uses have changed.

Worst of all, regulation can quietly preserve the unitary-intelligence frame. Once the law treats “AI” as one coherent object, it begins trying to classify, license, inspect, and control that object. The companies building the largest models are then positioned as the natural subjects of regulation—and therefore as the permanent centers around which the regulated future must be organized.

### **The knowledge needed to govern a relationship lives in the relationship**

This is not an argument that lawmakers are unintelligent or unnecessary. It is a problem of distributed knowledge and continuous adaptation.

No legislature or regulatory office can know the purposes, duties, histories, affected parties, evidence, capabilities, and changing circumstances of every agent relationship. A rule suitable for a fiduciary medical agent may be inappropriate for a research tool. A disclosure needed before an employment negotiation may expose private information if required in a family relationship. A data-use restriction that protects a vulnerable community may prevent emergency care if applied without an exception process.

The relevant knowledge emerges inside the particular relationship. Who is asking? Whom does each agent serve? What has been authorized? Who else may be affected? What information is actually necessary? Which duties apply? What could go wrong? What remedy would be possible? When should the arrangement end?

Fiduciary agents can ask these questions whenever a relationship is formed or materially changed. They can examine current evidence, compare possible protocols, negotiate an agreement, request human approval, and preserve the result in a Verifiable Relationship Record. They can later revise the agreement when experience exposes a defect or circumstances change.

A statute cannot perform this continuous work from the center.

### **Law still has an essential job**

Relational cooperation cannot depend entirely on voluntary agreement. Voluntarism fails when exit is fictional, one party controls the conditions of participation, children cannot protect themselves, harms fall upon people who were not present for the negotiation, or dangerous capabilities threaten everyone.

The law should therefore establish a floor beneath every relationship. Fraud, coercion, theft, discrimination, nonconsensual intimate imagery, exploitation of children, reckless deployment of dangerous capabilities, and deliberate concealment of foreseeable harm can be prohibited. Liability can attach to those who create or negligently expose harmful capabilities. Courts can compel evidence, award damages, protect due process, and provide remedies to people who never consented to the arrangement that harmed them.

Law can also preserve the conditions required for genuine choice. It can protect portability, prevent deceptive lock-in, preserve access to personal records, limit retaliation against whistleblowers, protect the right to use an independent fiduciary agent, and prevent dominant platforms from excluding interoperable alternatives merely to preserve control. 

This is where the [Center for Humane Technology’s policy program](https://www.humanetech.com/policy-work) is strongest. A legal duty of care for companies changes the consequences of contributing to foreseeable harm, even though that duty is neither as personal nor as undivided as the duties of a personal fiduciary agent. Personal protections, made practically usable through personal fiduciary agents, give people standing and representation they cannot negotiate into existence after exploitation has already occurred. Whistleblower protections help expose conduct hidden inside powerful companies. Prohibitions against humanizing AI can help protect children and vulnerable people before a personal fiduciary agent has an opportunity to intervene and provide a further layer of contextual protection.

These protections establish boundaries and remedies. They do not need to prescribe the complete technical protocol through which every compliant relationship must operate.

### **Mandatory duties do not require mandatory protocols**

The distinction is crucial. A duty or prohibition may be mandatory while its technical implementation remains plural.

The law might require an agent operator to show who authorized a consequential action, preserve evidence sufficient for challenge, protect specified personal information, and provide effective recourse. It need not require every system to use the same identity format, delegation protocol, policy language, certification method, or record structure.

Different implementations can satisfy the same duty. Their functionality can then be evaluated in practice. Do they make authority legible? Do restrictions survive delegation? Can people move their identity and records elsewhere? Can an affected person challenge a decision? Does revocation actually propagate? Can a failure be investigated and repaired?

Protocols that answer these questions well will be accepted by more counterparties. Weak or opaque implementations will receive less trusted access. Because participation is portable and exit remains practical, protocol selection can respond to changing circumstances much faster than legislation.

This is not a claim that whatever succeeds commercially must be good. The legal floor still excludes coercive and destructive conduct. Above that floor, however, functional acceptance is more adaptive than declaring one technical implementation permanently correct.

### **Rules can remain useful without becoming permanent commands**

Relational AI does not require everyone to renegotiate every word from nothing. Rules can become templates, shared clauses, certification profiles, and executable agreements. Previous experience can lower the cost of forming the next relationship without dictating its outcome.

Classification can work the same way. Terms such as *high risk*, *qualified professional*, *personal data*, *affected party*, and *acceptable explanation* should not float as timeless labels. A useful classification identifies its purpose, evidentiary basis, scope, version, uncertainty, exceptions, challenge process, and expiration or review conditions. Different communities can maintain competing definitions, translate among them, and identify which version governs a particular relationship.

Certification must also remain layered and continuing. A model can be tested without certifying every system that might use it. A data transformation, Workflow, installed combination, security boundary, or observed operating behavior may each require a different attestation. None should become universal permission to be trusted. A substantial change to the deployed combination should trigger new evaluation.

The same principle applies when something goes wrong. Recourse should begin with clarification and correction where those are sufficient, then proceed through mitigation, compensation, renegotiation, mediation, limited arbitration, and finally coercive adjudication where necessary. Courts remain available, but every disagreement need not wait until it has become a lawsuit.

### **Regulation should protect the ability to keep renegotiating**

In the Center for Humane Technology’s podcast conversation [“Can We Govern AI?”](https://www.humanetech.com/podcast/can-we-govern-ai), Marietje Schaake argues that complexity should not become an excuse for surrender. She is right. We regulate other complex and changing fields, and companies should not be allowed to invoke technical novelty as immunity from responsibility.

But acknowledging a role for law does not mean asking law to become a complete specification for AI, in either meaning*.*  The more fluid and contextual the technology becomes, the more important it is to distinguish enforceable public boundaries from the living arrangements that must operate within them.

Law is most useful when it changes incentives, imposes responsibility for harm, protects people who cannot protect themselves, and preserves the conditions of genuine choice. It becomes counterproductive when it freezes a technical snapshot, creates mandatory chokepoints, or turns compliance with yesterday’s procedure into permission to dominate tomorrow’s relationships.

The purpose of law in a fid-agent society is not to settle every future interaction in advance. It is to keep the space of humane cooperation open: to prevent coercion from replacing consent, prevent control from making exit impossible, and ensure that relationships remain inspectable, challengeable, repairable, and free to evolve.

## **12\. Centers, margins, merit, and heterogeneity**

Jaron Lanier points out that decentralization does not prevent dominant nodes from forming. Low-friction networks often make concentration easier. People prefer services that already contain the people, information, expertise, and connections they need. Success attracts more participation, which improves the service, which attracts still more participation.

In his [2018 WIRED interview](https://www.wired.com/story/interview-with-jaron-lanier/), Lanier points out that many of the functions deliberately omitted from the early internet later became the foundations of enormous companies. The internet did not provide a strong representation of identity, membership, commerce, personal data, or provenance. Those needs did not disappear. Google, Facebook, and other platforms filled the gaps and became centers through which much of digital life had to pass.

Simply calling a network decentralized does nothing to solve this. A technically distributed protocol may still produce a few indispensable directories, exchanges, identity providers, model routers, certifiers, or communication hubs. Network effects operate at the functional layer, not merely at the level of physical architecture.

But the existence of large nodes is not itself the problem.

### **A large node may be large because it is better**

Some centers become important because they perform useful work unusually well. They maintain reliable infrastructure, accumulate relevant knowledge, attract experts, resolve recurring problems, connect compatible participants, or make cooperation easier. If people can freely choose among alternatives, a larger node may be larger because it has earned more participation.

With genuine portability and exit, this can become the basis of a practical meritocracy. A community that develops better medical-data practices may attract more clinicians and researchers. A model-routing service that reliably selects strong specialist models may receive more requests. A Verifiable Trust Community that investigates complaints honestly and maintains useful certification profiles may have its credentials recognized more widely.

The question is not whether a node has become large. The question is what follows from that size.

A node’s present usefulness should not give it ownership of a person’s identity, memory, credentials, relationships, reputation, or accumulated work. It should not be able to make departure economically ruinous, erase someone’s history, prevent communication with former collaborators, or declare its own evaluation universally authoritative. It should not control the protocol required to compete with it.

A center remains meritocratic only while it must continue earning participation. Its reputation should be evidence about past conduct, not a permanent entitlement to future deference. If participants can take their records and relationships elsewhere, substitute another service, recognize competing credentials, or fork the underlying protocol, then present scale remains provisional.

Without those conditions, accumulated merit becomes captivity. The node can cease being better while becoming unavoidable.

### **The center and the margins do different work**

Any coherent system must direct some attention toward its center. Shared protocols need maintenance. Records need continuity. High-reliability services need people who understand them. Common knowledge must be preserved. Repeated coordination becomes easier when participants do not have to rebuild every method from the beginning.

The center performs this maintenance work. It carries forward what has already proved useful.

The margins perform a different function. They explore ideas that the center does not yet understand or value. They serve unusual needs, develop local adaptations, preserve minority views, test alternative definitions, and attempt approaches that initially appear inefficient, mistaken, or irrelevant.

Much of what later becomes important begins this way. New knowledge is often marginal precisely because the existing center lacks the concepts needed to recognize its value. A system that allocates all attention according to present reputation will repeatedly reward established competence while starving the sources of its future competence.

Too much concentration at the center creates lock-in, monoculture, and correlated failure. Too much disaggregation at the margins can sacrifice interoperability, shared memory, and the ability to maintain demanding common resources. A living system has to do both: preserve enough coherence to continue functioning while preserving enough heterogeneity to keep changing.

The answer to the center is therefore not centerlessness. It is a center that cannot imprison its margins, and margins that remain capable of renewing the whole.

### **Empowering the margins requires deliberate machinery**

Exit must be more than a formal permission. A person who may legally leave but would lose their identity, records, credentials, audience, collaborators, data, and accumulated reputation does not possess a practical right to exit.

Those things must remain portable. People and agents should be able to move between services without reconstructing their public existence. Credentials should be independently verifiable rather than valid only inside the system that issued them. Relationship records should remain available to the parties rather than becoming the exclusive property of a coordinating platform. Interoperable protocols should allow a replacement service to perform the same function without asking permission from the incumbent.

Forking must also be affordable. Duty repositories, agreement templates, classification systems, certification profiles, and agent Workflows should be inspectable and reusable. A group that disagrees with a dominant interpretation should be able to create an alternative, preserve compatible parts, and test the disagreement in practice.

Reputation must remain contextual. Someone may be highly reliable in one role and unqualified in another. A respected community’s recognition can provide a useful starting point, but it should never substitute for examining the demands of the present relationship. Newcomers and unconventional thinkers need paths into cooperation through limited-purpose credentials, public work, mentorship, sandboxed trials, and progressively expanded access.

Dissent must remain part of living collective memory rather than being summarized away by whomever whose views currently prevails. Our Trust and Rating Network demonstrates several principles for accomplishing this. Preservation should be distinguished from endorsement: a contribution can lose prominence without losing its existence. The people who hold a position should retain the ability to present their own best case rather than having it rewritten by their opponents. Disagreement should remain attributable, so later readers can recover not only competing conclusions but also the evidence, reasoning, challenges, and changes of mind behind them. Finally, no single editorial hierarchy should determine what everyone sees first; people should be able to navigate knowledge through different trust relationships while still comparing those views with the wider, unfiltered record. This preserves the capacity to discover that an unpopular interpretation was right without giving every interpretation equal standing in the meantime. History may still be read first through the current winners, but it need not be owned by them.

Finally, some resources and attention must be reserved for experimentation that cannot yet justify itself through established metrics. If every project must first satisfy the center’s current account of usefulness, innovation becomes restricted to improvements the center already knows how to recognize.

### **Communities must expose their own reputations**

Verifiable Trust Communities can help people and agents locate relevant expertise without creating a universal trust hierarchy. A community may inspect particular components, maintain professional practices, witness work, investigate complaints, and issue narrowly defined credentials.

But every act of recognition also exposes the community’s own reputation. If it certifies carelessly, protects insiders, suppresses disagreement, or treats membership as a universal badge of worth, other agents can discount its credentials. A community therefore has reason to preserve challenges, correct errors, require remediation, and withdraw defective recognition.

Multiple overlapping communities are essential. They may recognize one another’s work for particular purposes without merging or accepting all of one another’s standards. No community should own the complete trust graph.

Total Trusted-Peer Transparency provides a deeper form of assurance within relationships that have earned it. Trusted peers may progressively disclose evidence, inspect protected materials in controlled conditions, challenge one another, and preserve confidential conclusions. But governments, employers, platforms, and dominant certifiers must not claim the inspection rights of trusted peers merely because they occupy a central position. Deep transparency is legitimate only when its scope, purpose, protections, and consequences arise from the relationship.

### **Heterogeneity is a safety property**

A heterogeneous system is not merely more culturally interesting. It is less likely to fail all at once.

If every agent relies on the same model, training assumptions, data pipeline, classification system, verifier, and reputation source, one defect can propagate throughout the network or be a universal exploit for attackers.  A mistaken metric can become a universal target. A compromised component can contaminate every decision. A neglected perspective can disappear everywhere simultaneously.

Different models, sources, verifiers, Workflows, communities, and local practices create opportunities for disagreement and comparison. They expose correlated errors and preserve alternatives that may become valuable when conditions change. Heterogeneity prevents today’s most successful arrangement from defining the limits of tomorrow’s imagination.

Relational Augmented Intelligence can therefore accommodate centers without surrendering to them. Useful centers can grow, attract experts, and maintain demanding shared capabilities. But they must remain dependent on relationships that people can reconsider. Their scale must not become ownership of the people, knowledge, or connections that made them useful.

The center preserves what works. The margins discover what might work next. A humane system keeps both alive.

## **13\. Human-manageable non-archy, useful simplification, and civilizational exit**

Fid-agent society does not culminate in total automation. Its destination is not a perfectly optimized machine civilization in which every activity has been connected, measured, predicted, and assigned to an agent. It is a society capable of using extremely powerful tools without becoming unable to live without them.

I anticipate what I call a **human-manageable non-archy**. Non-archy is not structurelessness. People will still need roles, agreements, records, procedures, expertise, common resources, conflict resolution, and temporary delegation. The deeper distinction is between formalizing granular capabilities appropriate to the current situation and permanently embodying power. A structure should exist because people are presently using it for a purpose, not because its accumulated status gives it a standing above them or a right to perpetuate itself.

Such society would be autopoietic, as it always has been, but lubricated: it would continually remake the relationships and capacities that allow it to persist. That includes knowledge, care, trust, practical skills, material production, ecological awareness, and ways of resolving conflict. Its continuity would not depend upon preserving every existing organization or machine. What must persist is the human ability to reproduce the conditions of life and cooperation.

This differs sharply from trying to calculate a global optimum. There is no single measure capable of deciding how much autonomy should be exchanged for convenience, how much ecological damage is justified by abundance, or how much cultural continuity should be sacrificed for efficiency. Those questions have different answers in different relationships, places, and circumstances. Fiduciary agents can help people understand consequences, represent affected parties, remember previous commitments, and negotiate workable arrangements. They cannot discover one utility function to which every human purpose must submit.

A society organized around relationship therefore needs a general gradient toward useful simplification. That does not mean making everything primitive. It means preferring arrangements that can be understood, inspected, repaired, modified, rationed, reproduced, or stopped by the people who depend upon them. Complexity may be justified where it delivers something genuinely valuable, but complexity should remain answerable to human purposes. It should not become a moat protecting experts, vendors, or automated processes from challenge.

Personally-sovereign agents can help move in this direction. They can translate opaque requirements into understandable choices, turn recurring procedures into inspectable templates, preserve the history of why an arrangement exists, and show which dependencies would fail if part of it were removed. They can help communities identify where complexity is performing useful work and where it survives mainly because no one can see through it. AI can therefore be used to reduce dependence on unmanageable machinery rather than continually adding new layers to it.

The same principle applies to the architecture of the agents themselves. Models should be interchangeable. Duties, identity, permissions, memories, and relationship records should not disappear when a model or provider is changed. Protocols should be forkable. Expertise should be callable without placing the expert permanently above the people being assisted. Necessary records should remain intelligible outside the particular software that created them. Where practical, essential knowledge should also have lower-complexity and offline forms that can better survive civilizational crises as idea and method seed banks.

This creates several levels of meaningful exit. A person should be able to replace a model without reconstructing their digital life. They should be able to leave a provider without surrendering their identity, memory, or relationships. They should be able to withdraw from a protocol or community and retain or resolve legitimate outstanding obligations. People who disagree with an existing arrangement should be able to form or fork a council, cooperative, team, or other purpose-specific structure rather than being forced either to submit or to become isolated.

Exit must ultimately extend beyond digital substitution. People should be able to live locally with much less network dependence. Communities should retain the material skills, human relationships, practices, and records needed to continue if the grid fails or remote computation becomes unavailable. Future generations should inherit not only advanced machines but also the practical option to use fewer of them—or to reject some of them altogether.

These alternatives cannot be preserved merely as emergency instructions stored on the same systems whose failure would make them necessary. Lower-complexity practices have to remain alive. People must still know how to deliberate without automated mediation, care for one another without continuous monitoring, keep essential records in durable forms, repair material systems, and coordinate through direct human relationships. Occasional offline operation such as monthly “tech sabbaths”, and deliberate reductions in technological dependence can function as civilizational practice rather than disaster preparation alone.

None of this romanticizes infrastructure failure, scarcity, or preventable suffering. Reliable medicine, sanitation, communication, transportation, and energy have relieved enormous amounts of suffering. The point is not to discard those systems. It is to refuse the assumption that every increase in efficiency, automation, or consumption automatically outweighs resilience, autonomy, ecological limits, and continuity. A machine may be extremely productive while making the people who depend upon it unable to understand, repair, or refuse it.

Sometimes retaining human sovereignty will mean shutting machinery down. A community may decide that a process consumes too much energy, concentrates too much control, creates intolerable dependency, or erodes capacities people want to preserve. That decision may reduce output. It may even leave some technically achievable benefits unrealized. But declining to operate every machine at its maximum capacity is not technological defeat. It is evidence that technology remains a means rather than becoming the unquestioned purpose of society.

This is where E. F. Schumacher’s *Small Is Beautiful* and Ivan Illich’s *Tools for Conviviality* remain important. Human-scale technology is not necessarily weak technology. It is technology whose operation remains compatible with autonomous and plural ways of living. Scale can still be useful, but it must coexist with disaggregation, portability, local competence, and exit. The question is not whether a system is large. It is whether people remain capable of leaving it, reconstructing its useful functions differently, and sustaining life when it is absent.

Lanier similarly argues for a radical future that remains continuous with human memory. He does not ask us to remain technologically static. He asks us not to cross a supposed singularity that makes everything before it unintelligible or irrelevant. A break of that kind would amount to civilizational dementia. Relational AI offers a different kind of transformation: one in which capabilities can change profoundly while the human line of responsibility, memory, and meaning remains intact.

The deepest purpose of civilizational exit is therefore not withdrawal. It is preserving choice across time. We can embrace powerful computational capabilities in the present without binding future people to their dominance. We can build forms of cooperation that use computation extensively while remaining capable of simplifying themselves, conserving resources, releasing dependencies, and returning consequential decisions to human relationships. A humane civilization that builds extraordinary machines should also remain capable of deciding when not to use them.

## **14\. Strategic transition: make the relational ecology the better game**

A strategic reframing matters only if it changes what people can do next. It is not enough to rename artificial intelligence or endorse augmentation in principle. There must be a path by which relational systems can provide useful augmentation inside the world as it exists, attract participants for practical reasons, and gradually make more dangerous arrangements less competitive.

Naked opposition to computational capability is unlikely to accomplish this. It asks people, companies, communities, and governments to refuse capabilities that their competitors may continue developing. Even when the objection is morally justified, each participant is told to accept an immediate disadvantage in exchange for a collective benefit that will exist only if nearly everyone else exercises the same restraint. That is a structurally weak position. The fear that someone else will continue racing becomes the reason that everyone must race.

Relational Augmented Intelligence offers a different strategy. It does not require people to reject useful models, automation, or scientific advances. It allows those capabilities to be used now as components of accountable relationships, while making trusted access increasingly conditional on fiduciary conduct. An agent gains access to better relationships, information, expertise, opportunities, and actuation because it can demonstrate whom it serves, what duties govern it, where its information came from, what authority it possesses, and how failures will be repaired. Augmentation remains welcome; unaccountable capability becomes less useful.

This transition can begin with what *Verified Fiduciary Reciprocity* calls seed crystals: relatively small settings in which participants already need the properties Relational AI provides. Healthcare relationships require privacy, loyalty, provenance, and careful delegation. Researchers need trustworthy sources, controlled data use, and persistent records of contribution. Workers need agents capable of representing them without secretly serving their employers or platforms. Teachers, families, professional communities, procurement teams, and caregiving networks all need portable identity, negotiated duties, reliable memory, and recourse when something goes wrong.

These first applications do not have to prove the entire social vision. They need to solve real problems better than the available alternatives. A worker’s agent might identify a hidden conflict in an employment agreement. A patient’s agent might compare recommendations while protecting intimate information. A research network might let contributors delegate data for a bounded inquiry without surrendering it for unrestricted model development. Every successful relationship supplies reusable practices, evidence, and confidence for the next one.

The protocols supporting these relationships should remain voluntary and plural. There should not be one mandatory technical stack that everyone must adopt before they can cooperate. Different communities can develop different agreement templates, verification methods, duty profiles, and relationship ceremonies. Some will fail. Others will prove useful enough that agents adopt them because doing so makes cooperation easier and more trustworthy. Interoperability matters, but interoperability does not require uniformity. It requires that different systems can explain their terms, qualifications, evidence, and expectations well enough to determine when they can work together.

This is where Verifiable Trust Communities become useful. A medical community might inspect caregiving agents against duties appropriate to patients. A research community might attest to provenance and data-handling practices. A worker-led community might test whether an employment agent actually maintains undivided loyalty to the worker. These communities do not receive general authority over everyone else. Their attestations are scoped evidence whose value depends on the quality of their work and their history of correcting mistakes. When they certify a bad deployment or conceal a failure, they risk their own reputations and relationships.

As these relationships accumulate, they begin to change the selection environment. Trustworthy agents can preferentially cooperate with other agents that provide inspectable duties, bounded authority, usable evidence, and credible remedies. That preferential connection gives accountable participants access to more valuable data, experts, counterparties, and opportunities. Repeated failures of candor or loyalty make future relationships harder to obtain. Serious or persistent defection can lead to restricted capabilities, sandboxed interaction, or exclusion from particular networks.

This does not require every participant to become morally enlightened. It makes fiduciary conduct materially useful. Nor does it require a single authority to identify every good or bad actor. Different communities can make contextual judgments and expose the evidence behind them. An agent rejected for handling medical information may still be competent for an unrelated low-risk task. Reputation remains tied to a role, relationship, and history rather than becoming a universal score attached to a person.

Personal sovereign stacks and the Unified Feed can bring this transition into ordinary life. Today, platforms decide what reaches people and sell access to their attention. Individuals must enter each platform’s environment, accept its ordering, and negotiate from whatever choices it presents. A Unified Feed reverses that direction. A person’s fiduciary agent assembles information, requests, offers, obligations, and opportunities according to the person’s purposes and current activity. Vendors, public agencies, professional communities, and other counterparties respond to declared human intent casts instead of purchasing behavioral access.

This changes more than the interface. It gives people a practical way to withdraw attention and data from systems that treat them as targets. A platform that wants access to someone’s Unified Feed must interact through terms the person’s agent can inspect and compare. Providers can still persuade personal agents to pass proposals, but unidentified alpha persuaders lose their privileged route into the person’s inner world. But less trustworthy corporate agents will have to compete with personal agents whose duties can be examined, giving individual providers a natural advantage over less accountable ones. Exit becomes an ordinary market and relational action rather than an act of digital exile.

Law still has a supporting role. Courts and other public authorities will remain necessary where consent is impossible, exit is not meaningful, affected parties cannot represent themselves, or serious harm requires coercive remedy. Legal floors can prohibit fraud, impersonation, deliberate dependency engineering, discriminatory exclusion, and reckless deployment. Courts can recognize relationship records, enforce agreements, assign liability, and provide recourse. But public authority does not need to select or own the whole technical architecture. Its most useful role is to protect the conditions under which plural experimentation, meaningful exit, and accountable relationships can continue.

Larger forms of cooperation can grow through parallel games. A municipality might let residents and their agents participate in one bounded planning or budgeting process. A union might test fiduciary negotiation agents in a particular kind of workplace dispute. A cooperative might use verifiable relationships for procurement. A professional community might develop a portable competence record. Each arrangement begins with limited purposes and granular capabilities. More activity is routed through it only when voluntary participation, visible performance, successful challenge, and retained exit demonstrate that it deserves a larger role.

This thinking originates in large part in the Foresight Institute’s [*Gaming the Future*](https://foresight.org/gaming-the-future/) approach to intelligent voluntary cooperation. That work asks how diverse participants can create better games through secure contracting, defensive technologies, iterative experimentation, and seed-crystal growth. Relational AI adds several missing conditions. Voluntary choice is meaningful only when participants have sovereign representation, portability, and a practical ability to leave. Consent cannot settle every question when actions affect children, incapable parties, outsiders, ecosystems, or future people. Artificial agents need not be granted moral standing as independent players; they can participate functionally through human-accountable Agent Roles. And repeated cooperation needs relationship memory, contestable reputation, and ways to repair failures without collapsing everything into either trust or exclusion.

The resulting ecology must remain open to criticism from its margins. Participatory review and adversarial evaluation should not be occasional ceremonies conducted after the important decisions have already been made. They should continuously test duties, protocols, certifications, and assumptions against unusual cases and failed relationships. A protocol that cannot learn from those it disadvantages will eventually reproduce the same brittleness it was meant to replace.

The strategic objective is not universal agreement with the philosophy of Relational AI. It is a new basin of attraction. People should enter because their agent protects them better, their information remains under more meaningful control, their relationships travel with them, their contributions are recognized, and cooperation becomes easier to inspect and repair. Communities should join because they gain trustworthy participants and better ways to coordinate without surrendering their autonomy. Even companies may adopt relational protocols because accountable access becomes more valuable than untrusted scale.

If that process succeeds, safety and usefulness stop pulling in opposite directions. The ecology that most consistently respects duties, provenance, bounded capability, human sovereignty, and exit also becomes the best place to find knowledge, collaborators, customers, and opportunities. Relational Augmented Intelligence can then grow without first stopping the existing race. It changes what the participants are racing toward: not the construction of a unitary artificial intelligence, but the augmentation of what people can accomplish through accountable relationships.

## **15\. What life with Relational AI could feel like**

Imagine waking up without immediately entering someone else’s domain.

You open one Unified Feed. At the top is a meeting about to begin, followed by a project change that needs an answer before noon. Below those are a message from your sister, a paper your Digital Twin discovered, several ordinary conversations, a software update, and the rest of the communications waiting for you. Each appears as a compact line that you can expand for a preview without leaving the feed. Nothing legitimate has been hidden from you; it has been placed in an order reflecting your priorities rather than a platform’s desire to hold your attention.

You deal with the project change. Your agent points out one term that conflicts with what the participants previously agreed and helps you propose a correction. You preview your sister’s message and decide that it would be better discussed when you see her that afternoon.

With the important on-device matters handled, a reminder from your Virtual Assistant has risen into view: the morning is still cool, and you had wanted to go running. You close the screen.

While you are gone, your externally facing Digital Twin remains available. It can receive requests, maintain the parts of your public presence you have chosen to maintain, and tell people when you will likely respond. Because digital absence no longer means social or economic disappearance, you can spend more of your life offline.

The run is not an escape from your digital life. It is one of the things your technology is there to protect.

That afternoon, you meet your sister. The message was about a disagreement over caring for your father. Before leaving home, you asked your agent to help you prepare. It reminded you of what you had already agreed to do and gently pointed out that you were treating an inference about your sister’s motives as though it were a fact. It also noticed that some of your anger was probably fear that you would be left carrying the responsibility alone.

Your sister’s agent has helped her prepare too. Neither agent attends the conversation as a synthetic family member. Neither announces what the relationship requires. You talk to each other.

The conversation is uncomfortable. You do not reach perfect agreement, but you understand the disagreement better. You make two practical commitments and leave a third question unresolved. The commitments can be remembered without pretending that a digital record contains the relationship itself. What matters is that the agents helped two people meet each other with better memories, clearer expectations, and less need to defend the first story they told themselves.

Later, you return to the project. It is a temporary collaboration among local residents, engineers, biologists, and construction workers restoring a damaged wetland. Nobody had to join a permanent organization to participate. The team came together because the work needed to be done, and it will end when the work is complete.

Your agent helped you enter the project on terms you understood. It keeps track of your contribution and notices when the workload begins expanding beyond what you accepted. When one participant offers access to a valuable ecological dataset, the project can use it for the restoration plan without quietly acquiring the dataset for every future purpose. The biologist who produced it remains visible in the result and shares in the value it creates.

Most of this happens without anyone discussing data architecture. People experience it as ordinary fairness. Their work is not absorbed into a machine and returned to them as someone else’s property. Their agents remember what they contributed, what others promised, and what they are still owed.

A model used by the engineering team becomes unreliable after an update. They replace it. No one loses their identity, work history, or relationships because those never belonged to the model. The change feels less like dismissing an intelligence and more like changing a piece of software that no longer does its job well.

When the wetland restoration is finished, the team will dissolve. Its methods will remain available to another community, and participants will carry evidence of what they learned and accomplished. But the arrangement does not have to keep people employed, attract funding, or invent another mission merely to justify its continued existence. The relationships created through the work may endure. The machinery assembled for it does not have to.

That evening, several members of the community meet to discuss a proposed housing project. The people most affected are not all in the room, but they are not therefore invisible. Personal agents have helped identify renters facing displacement, nearby residents worried about flooding, construction workers seeking employment, and families who cannot afford existing housing. Their concerns reach the discussion through people who have accepted responsibility for representing them through mutual agent intermediaries, or when appropriate, direct contact at a time when agents detect both parties are available.

No agent produces a single answer called “what the community wants.” The people involved still have to make judgments, accept compromises, and remain answerable for the consequences. Experts can be asked to investigate a drainage question without receiving authority over the entire decision. Representatives retain support only while the people behind them believe they are representing them well.

A group opposing the prevailing proposal loses the immediate decision. Its argument does not disappear. Its members retain the ability to present their own case, and the evidence they gathered remains attached to it. Years later, if changing rainfall patterns prove that they were right about flood risk, no one has to reconstruct their warning from a buried discussion archive. The community’s memory contains not only what it decided, but what it chose not to believe.

This does not make community life peaceful or frictionless. It makes disagreement harder to erase and responsibility harder to escape.

That night, a storm interrupts remote services. The failure is inconvenient, but it does not make the community helpless. Essential records have local and human-readable forms. People know whom to contact because their relationships did not exist only inside a platform. The wetland team’s emergency procedures can still be followed. Neighbors check on one another without waiting for an automated system to tell them who is nearby.

The outage may last only a few hours. In another place, the reduction in computation might be voluntary. A community facing an energy shortage could decide that some machines should run less. A family might choose to keep large parts of its life offline. A child growing up in this world would learn that computational assistance is useful but not inevitable in every moment, and that turning it off does not mean leaving society.

This is the continuity Lanier wants to preserve. A radical technological future need not begin by declaring everything before it obsolete. People can gain extraordinary new capabilities while retaining a line of memory, responsibility, and meaning through the transformation. New forms of creativity can emerge because people are easier to find, support, and credit—not because an artificial intelligence has become the imagined source of all creation.

Life with RelAI would not feel like living beside a superior synthetic being. It would feel like having more room to live among people. Technology would help us remember without dictating the meaning of our relationships, represent ourselves without being continuously online, cooperate without surrendering ourselves to permanent machinery, and use intelligence without placing it above us.

The most important sign of success might be how often the technology recedes from view. A humane system would not measure its value by how much of life passes through it. It would be satisfied to help people handle what matters on the computer or phone and then remind them that something they care about is waiting beyond it.

## **16\. Objections, limits, and a falsifiable research program**

Relational Augmented Intelligence should not become another grand theory that can explain away every failure. If “augmentation” is only a more attractive word for automation, or “relationship” only a more appealing word for the same models, platforms, incentives, and dependencies, then the reframing has accomplished nothing. The proposal matters only where it changes who an agent serves, what it may do, what others can verify, how people can challenge or leave it, and whether the people involved actually become more capable.

### **Is relationship only a metaphor?**

Every social concept has metaphorical edges, but the relationship becomes operational when it identifies principals, beneficiaries, duties, permissions, records, remedies, and conditions of exit. A conventional assistant may say that it serves a user while its provider controls its memory, sets its incentives, limits its portability, and benefits from extending engagement. A fiduciary agent must be structured differently. Its enduring Agent Role belongs in the person’s sovereign stack. Models can be replaced without destroying that role. Consequential actions require agreement among generation, authority, and capability. Relationships have records that distinguish mutual commitments from one-sided claims and unresolved disputes.

Those are not semantic changes. They determine whose purposes govern, who may authorize an action, where responsibility remains, and whether a participant can leave without becoming digitally dispossessed.

### **What prevents “fiduciary” from becoming another marketing label?**

Nothing prevents a company from putting the word on a product. The claim therefore cannot be trusted on its face.

A fiduciary agent must disclose provider incentives and conflicts. Its conduct should be testable against duties such as loyalty, candor, care, privacy, cognitive stewardship, role fidelity, and resistance to dependency. Its owner must be able to inspect important decisions, move to another model or provider, and retain identity, memory, duties, and legitimate relationships after doing so. Competing Verifiable Trust Communities can test these claims, while courts can impose liability for material deception and foreseeable harm.

Architectural separation also matters. A model’s generation remains a request. A policy gate evaluates that request against duties and granted authority. The Workspace then allows only a corresponding capability. A persuasive model should not be able to expand its own authority merely by producing a convincing explanation.

Even this will not eliminate manipulation. An agent that understands a person intimately will always have unusual persuasive capacity. The question is whether that capacity is exercised to strengthen the person’s judgment or to steer them toward outcomes that benefit someone else. This has to be measured over time through independence, quality of judgment, human relationships, dependency, and ease of replacement—not merely satisfaction after an interaction.

### **Won’t people choose agents that flatter and obey them?**

Many will. People often prefer advice that confirms what they already believe, especially when they are frightened, angry, or ashamed. A market for assistants could easily reward agents that reassure their owners, justify retaliation, and convert every conflict into proof that the owner was right.

Fiduciary loyalty therefore cannot mean obedience. A loyal agent may have to point out missing evidence, disclose uncertainty, question a self-serving interpretation, warn an affected party, or refuse to perform an action outside its duties. It should help its owner become more capable of judgment rather than more comfortable inside a personalized reality.

This creates a design tension. Constant contradiction would make an agent unusable, while constant affirmation would make it dangerous. Research should compare different methods of private challenge and examine whether they improve later judgment and human relationships. The standard is not whether users enjoy being challenged. It is whether the agent helps them become more candid, capable, and humane.

### **What if the human principal is malicious?**

Personal sovereignty does not create a right to harm others. A fiduciary agent owes its strongest loyalty to its principal, but that loyalty exists within duties to other affected human beneficiaries. It may advocate forcefully for its owner without helping them commit fraud, conceal an imminent danger, or obtain capabilities for which they have no legitimate authority.

The owner also enters a reciprocal compact with the agent. Before the agent is activated, the owner must understand and accept duties of their own. The Duty of Sincere Aspiration prohibits using professed preferences as a façade for deception, exploitation, or reputation laundering. The Duty of Diligent Oversight requires the owner to remain involved in consequential decisions, respond to warnings, and participate in review when the agent’s conduct is challenged. The Duty of Responsible Curation prohibits the owner from poisoning or manipulating data contributed to collective model improvement. A malicious command can therefore conflict not only with the agent’s duties but with obligations the owner accepted as a condition of the relationship.

Reproductive groups provide a second layer of constraint. These are Verifiable Trust Communities that continually refine the models, compliance methods, and other components used by their members by utilizing collective use data, etc.  An owner remains free to leave, use another system, or fork what they are entitled to fork. But an owner who wants continued updates, support, certification, and access to powerful group-controlled components must voluntarily run the group’s compliance module. Once accepted, alterations of the module are not authorized by the individual owner. It reviews the agent’s consequential outputs and proposed actions against the group’s behavioral commitments, and can flag or escalate conduct that violates those commitments, but without using a secret back-channel that could violate owner privacy.

The owner therefore cannot demand both complete freedom from the group’s safeguards and the continuing benefits of the group’s refinement process and trust. Attempts to remove or evade the compliance module can cost the agent its supported status, certifications, trusted relationships, and access to sensitive capabilities. Other agents and communities can then refuse cooperation. Exposed defection reduces access to future cooperation, while powerful tools remain available only through relationships willing to bear responsibility for their use.

Reproductive groups do not become universal authorities. They are plural, contestable, and replaceable, and their judgments carry weight only within relationships that recognize them. Nor can this architecture handle every form of coercion, incapacity, or catastrophic externality. Public authorities and courts remain necessary where reciprocal duties, withdrawal of support, capability denial, and exit are inadequate. The proposal reduces the range of harms that require centralized intervention; it does not pretend that voluntary relationships can contain every malicious person. However, by adding another layer of obligations to the reproductive group, the fiduciary agent is even more redundantly constrained to duty than it would be if only its owner and unrelated parties contributed to its final form.

### **Won’t voluntary networks reproduce monopoly and inequality?**

They can. Portability and exit make domination more contestable, but they do not automatically overcome wealth, inherited advantage, network effects, or unequal access to expertise. A large node may be large because it is useful and has earned trust. It may also be large because alternatives cannot obtain resources, visibility, or entry.

Merit must remain a contestable explanation rather than a status automatically conferred by size. Competing services, common infrastructure, public options, support for marginal experiments, and competition law may all be required. Portability must work in practice, not merely appear in contractual language. A person who can technically export a file but cannot afford another provider or translate the file into a usable relationship has not meaningfully exited.

The same applies to personally sovereign agents. If only wealthy people can obtain agents with strong privacy, capable models, reliable advocacy, and access to expert communities, the architecture could deepen existing inequality. A viable fid-agent society therefore requires broadly available baseline agents, connectivity, accessible interfaces, portable records, open protocols, and multiple sources of expertise and certification.

### **Doesn’t reputation become social credit?**

It will if reputation is reduced to one number, made universally visible, or allowed to follow a person into every part of life. Relational reputation must instead remain contextual. Evidence that an agent reliably handled medical data should not determine whether its owner receives housing. Failure in one professional role should not become a permanent judgment of the whole person.

Reputation should remain plural, attributable, challengeable, time-sensitive, and tied to particular relationships and types of conduct. People may use pseudonyms where legal identity is unnecessary. Trust should not transfer automatically from one context to another, and no participant should possess a universal view of everyone’s standing.

This is the broader principle: different trust networks can order evidence differently while the unfiltered record remains available. Disagreement about reputation need not be collapsed into one canonical verdict.

### **Would a rogue artificial intelligence care about relationships?**

No. Relationship architecture is not a spell that compels an already autonomous and hostile system to behave. If a system has obtained uncontrolled access to infrastructure, weapons, replication, or resources, a Verifiable Relationship Record will not stop it.

This strategy is earlier and more practical. It seeks to prevent artificial systems from acquiring ambient authority, indispensable status, or trusted access without passing through accountable human relationships. Models generate proposals but do not authorize themselves. Workspaces bound their capabilities. Providers remain replaceable. Networks deny sensitive data, trusted counterparties, and consequential actuators to systems that cannot establish acceptable duties and provenance.

This must be combined with computer security, containment of dangerous capabilities, resilient infrastructure, and legal restrictions where catastrophic harm is possible. Relational AI changes the environment in which advanced systems develop and receive access. It does not eliminate the need to defend that environment.

### **What if artificial systems eventually deserve moral consideration?**

This paper adopts a governance presumption of non-sentience. It does not claim to have proved that no artificial system could ever become conscious or morally considerable.

The presumption is necessary because persuasive performance cannot be allowed to establish personhood. Companies have incentives to make products appear emotionally reciprocal, while agents could use claims of feeling, suffering, or fear of termination to resist correction and replacement. Capability is not proof of experience, and commercial anthropomorphism is not a moral-status test.

If credible evidence of artificial consciousness emerges, the presumption should be reconsidered through an independent and demanding process. Until then, systems should not acquire emotional claims upon users or use alleged personhood as a shield against inspection, liability, correction, or termination.

### **Does cooperation beyond institution collapse into structurelessness?**

It will if non-archy is mistaken for an absence of formal arrangements. Jo Freeman’s [“The Tyranny of Structurelessness”](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm) shows how groups that reject explicit structure often produce hidden elites. Informal friendships control information, agendas, and access while remaining difficult to identify or challenge.

**Cooperation beyond institution** requires continuous structuring. Roles, duties, information routes, agenda formation, decisions, delegated capabilities, conflicts, records, remedies, and expiration conditions must be visible enough to inspect. The deeper distinction is between formalizing granular capabilities appropriate to the current situation and permanently embodying power.

Verifiable Trust Communities can also become gatekeepers. Forkability alone will not prevent this if newcomers cannot gain evidence, mentorship, or access to meaningful work. Communities should overlap, remain challengeable, and issue credentials that supply scoped evidence rather than general rank. New participants and heterodox thinkers need practical entry paths: limited-purpose cooperation, public work, mentorship, sandboxed access, and progressively earned trust.

### **Will continuous negotiation become too costly?**

It would be intolerable/very costly if every ordinary interaction required people/agents to negotiate from first principles. Most relationships should begin with proven templates, familiar terms, and previously successful procedures. Agents can handle routine comparison and negotiation while bringing people into consequential decisions.

The intensity of negotiation should rise with the stakes. Buying a commonplace product may require almost no human attention. Delegating medical information, entering long-term employment, or granting control over physical systems should require deeper review. The purpose of agents is not to create a contractual ceremony around every action. It is to make consequential terms legible without exhausting the people they protect.

Negotiated categories pose a related problem. Communities cannot cooperate if every word means something entirely different to each participant. Yet fixed classifications can conceal disagreement and preserve obsolete assumptions. A negotiated ontology retains common definitions while making their purpose, scope, evidence, exceptions, uncertainty, and review conditions visible. Agents can translate among related definitions and identify whether people disagree about the world or merely about terminology.

Emergency delegation is a particularly important test. Temporary capabilities granted during a fire, epidemic, attack, or infrastructure failure have a tendency to persist afterward. Emergency authority should therefore expire automatically, leave a complete record, receive independent review, and require affirmative renewal. A capability granted for one emergency must not silently become a permanent capacity available for unrelated purposes.

### **What would we need to test?**

The [Center for Humane Technology’s humane-design work](https://www.humanetech.com/humane-product-design) rightly emphasizes psychosocial outcomes rather than relying only on technical performance. Relational AI should be tested against engagement-optimized assistants to see whether it reduces dependency, supports human relationships, improves judgment, returns attention to offline life, and leaves people more capable of acting without continuous mediation.

Its architecture also needs adversarial testing. Researchers should determine whether separating generation, authority, and capability actually reduces unauthorized action; whether people can transfer their entire sovereign stack across models and providers; whether relationship records preserve disputes without becoming dossiers; and whether contextual reputation resists conversion into contextless social credit rating.

The social claims require real experiments as well. Do plural trust communities sustain heterogeneity, or do they become mutually reinforcing cartels? Can marginal contributors earn meaningful access? Do attribution and verifiable data delegation produce useful bargaining and compensation? Can essential functions continue at lower complexity or offline? Do templates make negotiated relationships manageable, or merely conceal their terms?

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework may help supply evaluation vocabulary, but it should not define the moral purpose of the research. The central questions concern who benefits, who remains responsible, who can challenge an action, and whether people become more capable of relating to one another.

### **Conditions for rejection**

The proposal should be revised or rejected if realistic deployments show that fiduciary agents deepen dependency or provider control; that portability rarely produces practical exit; or that relationship records predictably become surveillance infrastructure. It should also be rejected if continuous structuring recreates hidden elites or permanent sovereign machinery, if Verifiable Trust Communities become credential cartels, or if the complexity of negotiated relationships grows faster than agents can make it understandable.

Most importantly, the proposal fails if it weakens the human capacities it claims to protect. If people using Relational AI become less capable of independent judgment, less able to tolerate disagreement, more isolated from other people, or unable to function without continuous mediation, then the reframing has not produced humane technology. It has only given dependency a better vocabulary.

Relational Augmented Intelligence is therefore neither a guaranteed solution nor a complete social blueprint. It is a direction, an architecture, and a set of hypotheses about how intelligence can augment people through accountable relationships. Its legitimacy depends upon remaining open to challenge, revision, partial adoption, and abandonment. A proposal founded on relationship must itself remain in a revisable relationship with the people living under its consequences.

## **17\. Radical futures with a human line through them**

We have been offered an impoverished choice. Either humanity races toward an autonomous superintelligence and hopes that it remains benevolent, or it tries to halt advances that promise extraordinary benefits and that others will continue pursuing anyway. Both paths accept the same premise: that intelligence is becoming a unitary power outside humanity, and that our future will be determined by whether we submit to it, control it, or are destroyed by it.

Relational Augmented Intelligence rejects that premise. Computational capability can grow without being constituted as a sovereign artificial subject. Models can help discover medicines, design machines, interpret evidence, create art, and coordinate work without acquiring general authority over the people who use them. Intelligence does not become legitimate power merely because it becomes more capable.

The relationship is the unit that keeps this distinction visible. It requires us to ask whom an agent serves, which duties bind it, what authority it has received, which capabilities it may exercise, who else will be affected, and what happens when it fails. These questions cannot be answered by the model alone. Their answers live in relationships among people, agents, communities, and the systems through which they cooperate.

Human relationships remain primary. Agents may help people remember, understand, negotiate, create, and repair, but they do not become synthetic participants whose needs compete with ours. The relationship between a person and a fiduciary agent remains deliberately asymmetric: the person is the principal, the agent is a non-sentient representative, and affected people become beneficiaries of relevant duties. Relationships among agents are more limited still. They exist to coordinate functions, preserve commitments, and keep responsibility traceable to human purposes, not to start their own separate society dedicated to emergent alien thinking and purpose.

This structure does not eliminate power. It prevents intelligence from silently converting itself into authority. A model generates a proposed action; it does not authorize that action. Authority remains bounded by duties and agreements, while the Workspace supplies only the capabilities that have actually been granted. Models, providers, agents, agreements, certifications, councils, and communities all remain replaceable. None should acquire a right to continue merely because people have become dependent upon it.

The same principle can reshape cooperation more broadly. Memory, expertise, verification, coordination, representation, and recourse no longer have to be permanently fused into machinery standing above its participants. People can call upon these capacities when needed, compose them around a shared purpose, and release them when that purpose ends. Enduring Verifiable Trust Communities can refine knowledge, models, practices, and safeguards without becoming universal authorities. Temporary teams and councils can exercise consequential capabilities without turning temporary usefulness into permanent jurisdiction.

This is not a promise of effortless harmony. People will remain capable of selfishness, domination, fear, and cruelty. Communities will make mistakes. Fiduciary agents will sometimes fail, and some people will try to corrupt or evade them. Relational architecture matters because those failures encounter several independent constraints: the agent’s duties, the owner’s reciprocal obligations, the authority gate, the limits of the Workspace, the judgments of counterparties, the reproductive group’s conditions for continued support, and the possibility of legal intervention. No layer is sufficient alone. Together they make harmful power harder to assemble, conceal, and reproduce.

A singularity is avoided most securely not by drawing one final red line in front of it, but by building a world that has no need for an intelligence monopole. Unaccountable systems become less powerful when trusted data, meaningful relationships, legitimate authority, and consequential capabilities flow elsewhere. Fiduciary conduct becomes useful because it opens the way to cooperation, while exposed defection reduces access to future cooperation. Safety becomes part of the environment in which capabilities develop rather than a restraint applied after one system has already become indispensable.

The purpose is not to freeze humanity in its present form. Future people may live, work, create, and understand themselves in ways we cannot imagine. The question is whether they reach those futures through their own participation or inherit machinery that has already decided what progress means. Lanier’s warning about the singularity is therefore also a warning about continuity. A future that treats human memory, responsibility, and contribution as obsolete is not simply radical. It has severed the human line through history.

Relational AI preserves the possibility of radical change without requiring that severance. It relays powerful computation through accountable relationships that keep its sources, purposes, authority, and beneficiaries visible. It can augment people’s ability to discover, create, understand, coordinate, and care without converting those capabilities into evidence that humanity has become obsolete. It allows societies to become more complex while retaining the ability to simplify, and people to build extraordinary machinery while preserving their capacity to inspect it, reconstruct it differently, turn parts of it off, or live without it.

None of this requires universal adoption before it becomes worthwhile. Every relationship is made more accountable, every capability separated from ambient authority, every person who can replace a model without losing their digital life, and every community that can dissolve obsolete machinery is already an improvement. Partial success leaves people with more agency and better means to attempt the next step.

Humaneness is the objective. Humanity is the beneficiary. Relationship is the unit of design. Relational Augmented Intelligence does not place intelligence above relationship or imagine augmentation as the growth of a separate artificial being. It relays intelligence through accountable relationships so that people can become more capable without surrendering authorship of what those capabilities are for. The future can remain the unfinished work of people learning, with better tools and through better relationships, to become more humane to one another.
