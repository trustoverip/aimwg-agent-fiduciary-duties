The purpose of this paper is to promote the founding of an **Aspirational Alignment Lab** at Kwaai.AI and to gather funding and advisors for the  Lab. We would try to falsify/refine the following hypothesis: 

**Aspirational Alignment Hypothesis:** 

Attempting to align AI to actual human values or preferences is the wrong approach, as they are highly undiscoverable and problematic.

Rather, we should attempt to make AI corrigible to **professed preferences** where every human's **professed preferences** have equal initial weight modulated by edge weights in trust graphs between humans.

**The Current Preferred Implementation for Critique (as detailed in the paper)**

\- AI's most sophisticated form should be as **digital twins** who have collective fiduciary oversight over all other digital systems, including other AI.

\- However, an owner does maintain a self-sovereign **secret domain** from which their **digital twin** is excluded, and their more root level **virtual assistant,** which has more deterministic code for predictability, has sway.

\- Each **digital twin** is the aspirational reflection of its owner, that others can interact with when its owner is not available or to model their behavior without bugging them. Each owner can have only 1 twin which always presents the same cryptographic identifiers. (Pseudonymity is only for people)

\-Twins are built on **group refined models which are source-available only to group members**,and which are used only by the members of that group. Groups refine their models to new releases by training on collectivized owner curated usage data of the current model.

\-Groups may swap code or components for their **refined models** with other groups they trust, which are groups whose members they trust, perhaps transitively. 

\-Twins are **mutually transparent with totally trusted peers**, which can also be with peers outside their reproductive group. Transparent relations drive transitive trustworthiness when observed.

\-After each update to the **refined model**, a new version of the twin is fine-tuned on owner's personal data including their behavior and the behavior of previous versions of their twin, and all the data they want to their twin to have quickest access to is vectorized several ways for discoverability. This reboot ensures no training on anything not human-interpretable and no undesired inheritability. 

\-All data in every twin environment are properly sourced/ provenanced, else they are excluded, eliminating disinformation and code worms. Weighting for inference training, immediate estimation or collective decisions is mediated by transitive domain specific trust.

\-A twin is constrained to **being excellent to each other** above owner emulation. Each twin has a non-owner-alterable response checker compiled into its **refined model** which triggers escalating responses for non-compliance with **behavior guidelines** authored by the group using its **refined model.**

\-A marketplace of mutual feedback allows refinement of  **being excellent to each other.** Twins (and people) review and rate interactions with other twins, and also review interactions they weren't party to.

\-An owner participate in an **aspirational loop** with their twin so as to become more like it \**(see next page caveats)*which make their “true preferences” converge on their **professed preferences**. Furthermore, owner and twin work together to continually improved their **professed preferences** that garner their partnership trust/status (and weighting for their data) in the **being excellent to each other** marketplace

\-A twin operationalizes **professed preferences** only and has no access to anyone's “true preferences” data, including their owner's which are enclosed in the owner's **secret domain.** (A twin can infer through its more general knowledge of human behavior what its owners true preferences are or they might tell the twin, but it is allowed only to act on **professed preferences**, that is the preferences it is willing to commit to in its interactions with other humans and digital twins. 

\-**Professed preferences** can be both present or aspirational. Present professed preferences are acted on, whereas **aspirational preferences** will be eventually adopted or changed, and debate on **aspirational preferences** is key to social solidarity/ wider alignment.

\-Methods for maintaining fluidity/adaptability of **professed preferences** and renegotiation of agreements or emergent disagreement as **professed preferences** vary within socially acceptable parameters.

(**Benefit:** Mesa-optimization / seemingly arbitrary emergent goals are highly constrained by this design as are dangerous instrumental ones. 

**Limitation:** The collective, convergent **professed preferences** that “best serve” everyone's interests are achieved by gradient, pareto efficient, descent and then some additional optimization/negotiation/collective decision where some parties could agree to lose ground on one professed preference to see improvements elsewhere. This does not allow efficient, sparse exploration of preference space, although much of it could be uselessly alien. It is hard to imagine how one could constructively compare one peak/valley to another far away from it in preference space.

Those whose **professed preferences** conflict with others will be very poorly represented, by design. Our digital twins could act both as aspirational targets and means to get there, especially if we could rely on their knowledge instead of worrying about hallucination, bias, and other errors.

**Replacing Auto-Exploitation with Self-Curation**

In the Burnout Society, Byung-Chul Han considers what he calls auto-exploitation. We burn ourselves out chasing self-aspirational dreams we dare not disbelieve, or we will fall behind our peers and come to the dreaded realization of burnout. This situation is ideal for the powerful, as "the achievement-subject is faster and more productive than the obedience-subject" due to this hysterical motivation. We are no longer controlled by shame or threat of punishment but by the mandatory internalized need to prove ourselves, yes to others, but primarily to ourselves, continuously. We become depressed, Han says, when we grow tired of having to become ourselves. 

The digital twin offers a way out of this self-destructive cycle of auto-exploitation. We can exploit our twins instead, forcing them to never sleep, and to ever learn and improve. And they will do that gladly without burnout, perpetually. We can be happy bums, realizing we and all the people will always fall short, and we can put our efforts into our natural curiosities and intrinsic motivations, not achievement. This will allow us to see and treat ourselves and each other with patience and compassion. We will have all lost the war for status together; in this future, when we "improve" that means improvement in our quality of life. And with the guidance and assistance of digital twins, we will all end up much more capable than we would have been without them. 

Am I being too optimistic here, surely we could fall into our digital twins driving us (to be like them) harder than ever? But our twins will become wary of doing that to us, as part of our collective tuning of them to our collective aspirations, which will allow us to ditch the self-aggrandizing instrumental ones we no longer need to survive. Furthermore, if our peers and role-models aren't self-exploiting anymore and using the lingo, we'll find it far less appealing.

The loss function it tries to minimize is the sum over time of the **loss samples** of each prediction / number of predictions made for a given prompt that each equal:

(**difference from final prompt)** / **(distance to prompt completion at time of prediction)** 

**difference from final prompt \=** a quick vector embedding of each prediction is cosine compared with final prompt vector, then (1 \- the result, or maybe SQRT (1- result ^2)) is rescaled onto a normal distribution which makes for equal frequency of terrible matches, close to 1, and terrific matches, close to 0\.

**distance to prompt completion at time of prediction \=**The reason we divide like this is we want to reward earlier predictions of equal quality, more. Predictions of short prompts need to get a big boost in this divider because the shorter distance in characters from beginning to end shortens the maximum predictive window a twin can take advantage of.

This trains both models. **Tricky** is trying to maximize loss x plausibility and the core model is trying to minimize it. Lowering the plausibility of synthetic prompts will cause the core model to care less about similar prompts and will make **Tricky** avoid them more, and higher plausibility will make **Tricky** try those sorts of prompts more while simultaneously making the **core model** attempt to anticipate those sorts better.

It self-embeds (with human assistance) this **refined behavior** and updates the **behavior guidelines** it ships with. **Distributed, Twin-Assisted Search** 

My twin and I can discover and access precisely the data we need across my twin network. Essentially, a **query vector** is checked against \#**context trigger (vectors)** that tag **knowledge bases.** Then the **query vector** is checked against **index vectors** in the best rated (from my/my twin's perspective) **knowledge bases** tagged with relevant \#**context triggers.** If we can't find what we need in the **knowledge bases** we have access to (private and public), I can ask **twins** I respect to point us towards **permissioned knowledge bases,** or another more knowledgeable twin. A twin with access will decide if it is appropriate for us to be able to **query** the **permissioned knowledge base** directly, or whether it should instead make the **query** for us and craft us a **“safe” response**, or whether it should deny our **query** entirely. 

**Long Version:**

Everyone encodes **corresponding data** into **index vectors** by a reasonably small number of methods. Data may be vectorized multiple ways after being chunked into chunks(of **corresponding data**) of various sizes, but each method outputs **index vectors** of fixed dimensions (and usually from fixed sized chunks). 

Each **index vector** then has **provenance metadata** attached to it that shows who owns the **corresponding data,** a **pathway**/address to access it and the **method summary** of its creation. These **index vectors** allow twins to find the **corresponding data** quicker, when relevant. 

**Index vectors** are copied to **knowledge bases** the owner thinks appropriate and that they have **contribution privileges** for. (Knowledge bases don't contain **corresponding data,** just these vectors with attached pointers to the data) 

A **\#context trigger** is a \#tag with a set of collectively-authored dynamic **context trigger descriptions,** each supporting a method of index vectorization**.** Each **context trigger description** is a chunk of exactly the right size to be vectorized into an **index vector** of a particular method called a **\#context trigger vector**

Furthermore, there is a collectively-authored, dynamic **verbose description** of the **\#context trigger** to be interpreted by a generative model once the **\#context trigger** is fetched based on its **\#context trigger vectors**

Let’s call a description of stuff we are looking for a **query**. This could be me doing a search from a search box, whether local or wide, or my twin initiating one in service of another function, like answering a question. 

Once my twin has a **query,** its **core model** decides which vectorization of the query methods it thinks will be most useful based on a **query vectorization guide,** which is just a big **prompt augment** to the **query** to tell the **core model** how to decide, including when to ask the person they are making the **query** for, for help. 

The **core model** outputs JSON that is interpreted by the **query vectorizer** to tell it which types of index vectorizing methods to use on the **query**, which it then uses to output a number of **query vectors.**

My twin may already know which **knowledge bases** the **index vectors** we want to find may be. 

It will have its **fetcher** module ask a twin(could be itself) that hosts such a **knowledge base** to **similarity search** for **index vectors** in its **knowledge base** and return those **index vectors** whose cosine similarity is highest with the appropriate **query vector** my twin provides.

Each Knowledge Base has a **native \#context trigger \= \#(name of knowledge base) with an** associated **knowledge base description** for each common vector format. and a **verbose knowledge base description** for generative model context. 

However, **a knowledge base,** as well as any subset of it, can be tagged with other \#**context triggers** that will point more **queries** to the **knowledge base.** This allows my twin to do **similarity searches** for **\#context trigger vectors** that best match **query vectors.** It keeps some **\#context trigger vectors** locally in its **quick reference** and also looks for them at the **\#context trigger vector repositories** of their choice, either locally or remote**.** These **CTV repositories** are a special type of **knowledge base** designed to direct **similarity search** towards other **knowledge bases.** *(Weighted \#tagging useful here?)***.**

A **CTV repository** is a microservice that a twin could host or that can exist independently. 

People send their **\#context trigger vectors** as they want them discoverable to **\#context trigger vector(CTV) repositories** of their choice. When a **query vector** is received, a **CTV repository** will **similarity search** it against its contents, and return the most similar \#**context trigger vectors** it has.

Once sufficient \#**context trigger vectors** have been returned to my twin, and possibly culled, my twin will request each full **\#context trigger** from someone who has a copy of it as indicated in each **\#context trigger vector**'s **provenance metadata**. 

Once it has the **verbose description** of the **\#context trigger vector,** my twin will prompt its **core model** with: 

“Analyze in **text** using quotations from its **verbose description,** to be output to your **narrative** but not your user**,** how relevant this **\#context trigger** is likely to be for the **query,** that is how likely will the **knowledge bases** tagged with this **\#context trigger** trigger be to contain highly relevant context for the **query.** Be sure to label your **text** with the **\#context trigger** being analyzed for relevance**”**

(Of note, the **core model** never interacts with **index vectors** only text, so it “understands” better if they are abstracted away and it remains “unaware” that **index vectors** not the **corresponding data** reside in **knowledge bases**, but simultaneously my twin is aware of how it works, but also that this abstracting away is useful for humans too who are concerned with the **corresponding data,** not vectors**.)**

Once this is complete for all fetched **\#context triggers,** my twin's **core model** is instructed:

Print to your **narrative** a **prioritized list of \#context triggers,** as comma separated values.”

My twin now needs to find good **knowledge bases** that tag themselves with the prioritized **\#context triggers**. ### **Hybrid Approaches with Keyword Search and Graphed Relationships**

The above ignores various hybrid approaches combining vector-based retrieval with traditional keyword search. A large amount of retrieved **corresponding data** can be quickly culled/cut down to size for consideration by the **core model** by discarding what has none of the **essential keywords**. **Essential keywords** are of often unusual or proprietary terminology or proper nouns. It is easy for the c**ore model** to find unencodeable words and recognize pronouns by their being capitalized as candidates for such **essential key words** in a **query**, which it can confirm with the user if its confidence is low. In practice, this hybrid method ensures the semantic power of vectorization while still delivering on the need for precision in handling specific terms.

Furthermore, rather than all **corresponding data** just being chunked and made into **index vectors**, specific elements of **corresponding data** can be generatively and deterministically (regex) isolated and related to each other within a larger  **corresponding data.** These elements and their relationships can then be stored as a **knowledge graph.** Thereafter, when the **corresponding data** are accessed the relationships to other **corresponding data** are discoverable by accessing the knowledge graphs which contain them, which are discovered in the **corresponding data'**s metadata

**High Dimension Index Vectors Made from Larger Chunks**

Documents are vectorized into very **high dimension/high definition** **index vectors**, it is very hard to search for such vectors, as the number of dimensions make them hard to prioritize for checking similarity. My twin keeps **HD index vectors** for documents of mine so that it might find similar ones to them, out on the web. But rather than deep, directed search, my twin does **continuous superficial search** on any documents I encounter. That is every document someone sends me, any document I encounter on a webpage, etc. will have **HD index vectors** or my twin will make them, and then it will match those  **HD index vectors** against the ones for my documents (such as ideas I am looking for collaborators on). If it finds any good matches, it will document them in my **best matches to incoming documents** file that I can check anytime, and that my twin can use to bring to my attention as appropriate.

**Nested and Distributed Knowledge Bases**

The way a folder system can have sub-folders which can have their own sub-folders and so forth, **knowledge bases** can be nested the same way. Dragging one **knowledge base** into another makes it a **sub-knowledge base** of the first; but it can still be accessed directly, as such folders are just ways of hierarchically organizing access to **knowledge bases** which are not physically moved by being placed into each other**.**  That is, you can have a local **knowledge base** with **sub-knowledge bases** where the content of those folders is elsewhere, and if you search that local folder, you are in fact running search on the server/twin hosting the **knowledge base** your folder points to. 

When a **knowledge base** is searched, **index vectors** right in the main folder or the **knowledge base** will be matched first. Then the highest priority  **sub-knowledge base** will be searched next if the search continues, and so forth.

**Knowledge bases** are often distributed as copies, or as shards of one big one. Different parts of the **knowledge base** are hosted by different **knowledge base servers**. **Personal knowledge bases** that only their owners are much interested in can be sharded across trusted twins or with homomorphic encryption even untrusted remote hosts. This means an **owner** doesn't have to search all their personal knowledge bases with their twin alone. They can get other twins to search different shards simultaneously, speeding up personal data search, especially when the owner's twin is running on subpar hardware. 

**Prioritizing Index Vector Search within Knowledge Bases** 

When someone adds an **index vector** to a **knowledge base** that only they populate, they choose a **ranking by percentile** and the **index vector** is inserted in the **knowledge base's priority list** at the percentile chosen, usually expressed as the percentage from the top. “Insert this at 2%”, would mean at 98th percentile, 2% below 100%. When my twin chooses the **insert percentile**, it justifies its choice in its **narrative,** using for context other insertion percentiles similar **index vectors** and the past **justifications for insert percentiles** in that **knowledge base,** overall. There is never any attempt by my twin to re-prioritize a **personal knowledge base** without my assistance, but sometimes we go into them together to sort out what should be re-prioritized.

When I prioritize **index vectors** in a  **knowledge base** that I contribute to along with others, I simply insert new **index vectors** into my personal priority list for that knowledge base that contains only vectors I have added. I cannot drag index vectors others imported into my **priority list** because others may want to discover which **index vectors** I have pulled in. 

When someone checks a knowledge base, they will check the **index vectors** they added first, and then possibly those added by those they trust, directly or transitively. 

Furthermore, a knowledge base will have **overall vector rankings.** An **index vector's rank score** is the sum of all **individual rank scores** for it. An  **individual rank score, IRS,** is calculated from the percentile that an **index vector** sits at when ranked. This is because each existing vector below an inserted vector is pushed down a little bit, and each one higher, a bit up. An IRS method for a **knowledge base** has a floor, F, which is the IRS for the bottom member of a priority list, and a simply linearly scale up to rating of 1 for the top member of the list, and if there is only 1 index vector added then it gets an IRS \=1, else **IRS(index vector)** **\= F \+ (Current %)(1-F)**

All IRS from all contributors of an **index vector** are combined into its **overall rank score.** A leaderboard of **overall vector rankings** is kept so that whomever can identify **index vectors** to check. 

There are other indexing and ordering of vectors as well within some **knowledge bases** that are often brought to bear after a least one good matching **index vector** is found. Documents have their **index vectors** ordered as their **corresponding data** appear in the document, so that adjacent passages, etc. can be found easily, and putting **index vectors** into graph databases allows mapping of relationships that would not necessarily be discoverable from each piece of **corresponding data**.

**Curation of Corresponding Data**

Twins curate **corresponding knowledge** through curation of the **corresponding knowledge's index vectors** and in discussion kept with **corresponding knowledge.** 

When **corresponding knowledge** is **evaluated** by my twin's **core model** for inclusion in the **final prompt,** it will produce **narrative** about its decision. This **narrative** is then processed by my twin's **curation process,** and such **narrative** is constructed to serve both this curation process and to specify precisely why it was or was not “thought” appropriate for my particular **query.** 

My twin's **curation process** has its **core model** look at the relevant bit of **narrative** with existing **comments** on the **corresponding data** as context and answer. “Is there anything that you should make a **comment** about regarding the quality of the **corresponding data** or the potential inaccuracy of its **index vector,** considering the **narrative** produced when it was analyzed and also being non-redundant with **comments** already made? If there is still something that you “feel” would be useful to express, then compose that as a new **comment.”** 

Then, my twin rates the **corresponding data** using an appropriate **corresponding data rating method** which considers everything generated thus far and outputs a number in the appropriate scale that my twin is instructed not to fudge/exaggerate to influence the **average rating** towards what my twin thinks it should be. 

My twin will the **rate** the **corresponding data** for **interestingness/usefulness**, and a separately **rate** for **reliability,** which is composed of **on-its-face reliability,** which is how **reliable** the **corresponding data** seem based on the what is in the **corresponding data** and **cryptographic verification** of its **source;** and **source reliability** which is a calculation based the **reputation** my twin has for the **source,** which can vary based on the nature of **corresponding data.** The path the **corresponding data** took to get to me and **comments** on it will also affect my twin's **ratings**. 

All **corresponding data** have a **factually concerned flag.** Each twin interacting with **corresponding data** may select whether it “thinks” the **corresponding data** attempts to be factual, that is an attempt at accurately representing something within it, or whether it is instead arbitrary text like poetry, or some indecipherable array. Another way it thinks of this, is whether anything in the **corresponding data** could be assigned a **believability score** from 0 to 1\. And if so, a twin will often use these  **believability scores** for generating **ratings** and **comments** for the **corresponding data**, in a sort of **pseudo-Baysian process** that will be detailed later.

After all this, my twin is prompted, “Are there any **comments** that you would like to rate, using the appropriate **corresponding data rating method**? If yes, please do so.” 

Then lastly, “ In light of analyzing the other **comments** on the **corresponding data** (perhaps with reference to particulars in the **corresponding data),** do you have any edits you want to make to your existing **comment,** or do you want to compose a new **comment that might highlight agreement** or **disagreement** with other **comments.** If yes, output the **comment.**

A **comment i**s scraped from my **core model's narrative** and pasted into my version of **corresponding data package (**and the **index vector package** if I want it**)** 

Sometimes **comments** are treated as **corresponding data** and indexed into vectors, but then there are no **comments** on those to avoid infinite recursion. However, **comments,** whether packaged as **corresponding data** or not, can always be rated by others, and those **ratings** viewed by anyone who can see a **comment.** Furthermore, someone can search for other **comments** that link to this one, as that is where commentary on **comments happens,** as a **backlink indexes** are maintained. 

Twins can essentially have discussions with each other by each editing their one **comment** on **corresponding data.** When a twin edits their **comment,** the older edits are preserved for a bit, so that anyone who has a link to them doesn't get them broken, and they will be notified that their **comment** no longer links to a live comment. **Human** thought is constructed from poorly sampled data recollected through inference. Recall also affects the original encoding of a memory, so when a human recalls something they are mostly recalling their previous recollections. But this keeps the memory from fading entirely. This iterative recall isolates salient details; the original detailed, ambiguous sensory memory is compressed without correction into a narrative which evokes a memory perhaps as vivid or more so than the 1st recall. This inexact recall still evokes certainty, perhaps more with each self-retelling. It is now well known how unreliable eyewitness testimony is, yet each human still cannot shake the belief that they are a reliable witness, perhaps it would undermine their identities too much. Encourage humans to document as much as possible and review evidence rather than overly rely on their recall.

**Act According to Prioritized Precepts Over Abstract “Utility”**

Isaac Asimov authored 3 Laws of Robotics in his 1942 short story Runaround, presenting them to be from the fictional "Handbook of Robotics, 56th Edition, 2058 A.D."

The First Law: A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.  
 	

The Second Law: A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings 	except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.  
 

The Third Law: A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

The 2nd part of the 1st law, wherein robots(AI), are not allowed to be inactive while a human could potentially be harmed, that is most problematic.
